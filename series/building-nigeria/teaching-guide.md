# HOW TO USE THIS GUIDE

## What this is

This is a **ready-to-speak teaching guide**. Every day is written so you can stand in front of a camera, or a classroom, or your own children at the dinner table, and deliver it almost word for word. You do not need to prepare anything else.

Each day runs **5 to 7 minutes** on camera and follows the same six-part shape:

| Section | Time | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The Hook** | 30 sec | A question or a fact that makes people stay |
| **2. The Word** | 45 sec | Theme Scripture + the day's Psalm 119 verse |
| **3. The Story** | 2 min | One true Nigerian story, told properly |
| **4. Two Truths** | 2 min | The teaching — two points, no more |
| **5. The Build** | 45 sec | One concrete thing to do today |
| **6. The Prayer** | 20 sec | Short, spoken, personal |

After the six sections, each day also carries three things **for you, not for the camera**:

- **FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10)** — a simplified retelling and one question, for family devotions, Sunday school, or a Shorts cut
- **BEYOND NIGERIA** — one sentence that lifts the day out of Nigeria and into any nation, so a viewer in Ghana, Kenya, the UK or the US can apply it
- **ACCURACY NOTE** — what is safe to say, what is *not* safe to say, and why

## Why the Accuracy Notes exist

This series was fact-checked line by line before it was written. **Twenty-two of the thirty original stories needed correction**, and six needed replacing outright. Some of the corrections are small (a date, a currency). Some are serious: one story was about a man who is very much alive, one quoted a famous line by the wrong author, one repeated a claim that Nigerian fact-checkers have formally ruled false.

The Accuracy Notes are there so that **nothing in this series can be taken apart in a comment section.** A nation-building series whose anchor verse is about righteousness cannot afford to be caught overstating. Read the note before you record each day. A full list of what changed and why is in the companion *Accuracy & Sources Report*.

## Three teaching rules for this series

**1. Tell the whole story.** Several of the people in this series were not Christians. One was an outspoken atheist. Two were Muslims. Some of them did things you would not want your children to copy. **Say so.** A viewer who knows something you left out will stop trusting everything else you said. And Scripture is entirely comfortable with this: the Good Samaritan was the wrong man doing the right thing (Luke 10:25–37), and Jesus told the story that way on purpose.

**2. Do not preach Nigeria into despair or into denial.** Neither "nothing works here" nor "everything is fine." Every day in this series names something that went wrong *and* something a Nigerian did right. That is the shape of the whole month.

**3. Righteousness is not a formula for wealth.** Proverbs 14:34 is proverbial wisdom about national character, not a machine that turns prayer into GDP. Teach it as *what genuinely lifts a people* — over against oil, arms, or reputation. Say that out loud at least twice during the month.

## A note on Scripture translation

This series uses the **NIV (2011)** throughout, with one deliberate exception: **Psalm 127:1 on Day 1 is read in the King James**, because that cadence is well known to Nigerian congregations. When you read it, say so: *"in the old King James…"*

Every Scripture in this guide has been checked. Where the original draft had quoted a different translation without saying so, it has been conformed to the NIV. **Before you record, read the day's two verses once from your own NIV Bible** — this guide is a script, not a Bible.

## Psalm 119 — why it runs through all thirty days

Psalm 119 is the longest chapter in the Bible: **176 verses**. It is an **acrostic** — 22 stanzas of 8 verses each, one stanza for every letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and every verse in a stanza begins with that letter. And almost every one of those 176 verses contains a word for God's revelation: *law, word, promise, statutes, precepts, decrees, commands, laws.*

Put those two facts together and you have the psalmist saying: **God's word, from A to Z, covering everything.** That is the spine of this series. Say it on Day 1 and again on Day 30.

# DAY 1 — INTRODUCTION

## "Why Nations Need Building, Not Just Governing"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 1 · Building the Nigeria God Intends*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

Nigeria struck oil in 1956. Seventy years later, more than half of us are poor.

Now — hold that. Because most people hear that and immediately start looking for someone to blame. A government. A tribe. A colonial power. And I want to suggest to you, on this first day, that if blame could have built Nigeria, Nigeria would be finished by now. We have had seventy years of blame. We have not had seventy years of builders.

So for the next thirty days, we are not going to govern Nigeria. We are going to **build** it. And we are going to start with the only piece of Nigeria you actually control — you.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Psalm 127:1 (KJV):**
*"Except the Lord build the house, they labour in vain that build it."*

**Psalm 119:73 (NIV):**
*"Your hands made me and formed me; give me understanding to learn your commands."*

Notice what those two verses do together. The first one says: without God, all your building is wasted effort. The second one says: God made you with His own hands, and He is willing to give you understanding if you ask. So the answer to "except the Lord build the house" is not *stop building*. It is *ask Him for understanding, then build*.

### 3. THE STORY

There is a town in Bayelsa State called **Oloibiri**. In 1956, Shell struck oil there — the first commercial oil in Nigeria's history. Two years later, in 1958, the first shipment of Nigerian crude left for the world.

Oloibiri produced roughly twenty million barrels of oil. The field was abandoned in 1978.

Today, Oloibiri has no piped water. It has no reliable electricity. The place where Nigeria's oil story began is poorer than places that never had a drop.

And the country? Nigeria is Africa's largest oil producer. And the World Bank estimated in 2025 that **more than half of all Nigerians live in poverty** — over a hundred million people. Among the world's major oil producers — the countries pumping more than a million barrels a day — Nigeria has the lowest income per person.

So let us be very clear about what our problem is not. It is not oil. We have oil. It is not land. It is not people — we have some of the most enterprising people on this earth. Something else is missing.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: A nation is not built by what it has. It is built by the character of the people who steward what it has.**

Proverbs 14:34 does not say *"oil exalts a nation."* It does not say *"population exalts a nation."* It says **righteousness** exalts a nation. Right dealing. Integrity in the small transaction. Justice for the person who cannot pay you back.

Oloibiri is what happens when a nation has the resource and not the righteousness. Everything we look at this month will be a variation on that one sentence.

**Truth Two: God formed you for understanding — not just for existence.**

Psalm 119:73 says *"Your hands made me and formed me"* — and then, immediately, *"give me understanding."* The psalmist does not assume that because God made him, he automatically knows how to live. He asks.

That is the posture of a builder. Not *"the government should have told me."* Not *"someone should have taught me."* But *"Lord, give me understanding."* Nation-building starts the moment one person stops waiting to be supplied and starts asking to be taught.

### 5. THE BUILD

Here is your assignment for today, and it takes two minutes.

Write down, somewhere you will see it again, the answer to this question: **What is one thing in my hand right now — a skill, a job, a shop, a classroom, a household — that Nigeria would be better off if I did it with excellence?**

Do not write ten. Write one. We are going to come back to it on Day 30.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, this month, do not let me be a passive citizen. You made me and formed me — now give me understanding. Make me a builder, one whose life You are not labouring in vain to build. In Jesus' name, Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
There is a town in Nigeria where oil was found a long, long time ago. Everybody thought that town would become the richest town in Nigeria. But today the people there still have to walk to fetch water. Having something good is not the same as looking after it well. God gave each of us something. He wants us to look after it well.
*Ask them:* **What is one thing you are good at? How could you use it to help somebody this week?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Every country has an Oloibiri — a place or a people who had the resource and never got the blessing. Name yours.

**ACCURACY NOTE:**

- **SAFE TO SAY —** oil found in commercial quantities at Oloibiri in **1956**; first export **1958**; field abandoned **1978** after roughly 20 million barrels; Nigeria is Africa's largest crude producer; **World Bank (2025) estimates 52.5% of Nigerians in poverty**.
- **CAUTION —** Do **not** say "one of the poorest oil producers on earth" — South Sudan and Chad are poorer and both produce oil. Say **"among the world's major producers — those pumping over a million barrels a day — Nigeria has the lowest income per person."**
- **CAUTION —** Do **not** give a month for the 1956 discovery. January and June are both claimed. Say "1956."
- **CAUTION —** Do **not** quote a GDP-per-capita figure. Nigeria rebased its GDP in mid-2025, so figures published before and after are not comparable and a critic will catch the mismatch. The poverty rate is the safer number.

# PART ONE: INTEGRITY & TRUTH-SEEKING

# DAY 2

## "The Woman Who Gave the Money Back"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 2 · Integrity & Truth-Seeking*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

I want to tell you about a woman who was given money she was legally entitled to keep — and who gave it back. And I want you to notice that it was that one act, years before anybody knew her name, that put her in the position to save thousands of Nigerian lives.

Integrity is not a thing you produce on the big day. It is a thing you have already been practising, or you have not.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Leviticus 19:11 (NIV):**
*"Do not steal. Do not lie. Do not deceive one another."*

**Psalm 119:30 (NIV):**
*"I have chosen the way of faithfulness; I have set my heart on your laws."*

Look at how short Leviticus 19:11 is. Three commands, nine words. God did not feel the need to explain it. And look at the verbs in Psalm 119:30 — *"I have chosen"* and *"I have set."* Both of them are things the psalmist did on purpose. Faithfulness did not happen to him.

### 3. THE STORY

In the late 1990s, a Nigerian university lecturer named **Dora Akunyili** was working as a zonal secretary at the Petroleum Trust Fund. She was ill, and the Fund approved money — around seventeen thousand pounds — for her to have surgery in the United Kingdom.

She travelled. And the doctors told her she did not need the surgery.

Now: the money had been approved. It was in her hands. Nobody was watching. She spent about five thousand pounds on the treatment she actually received — and she brought roughly **twelve thousand pounds back** and returned it.

The chairman of the Fund at that time wrote to her personally about it. And a few years later, in April 2001, that reputation is what put her name in front of a president looking for someone to run **NAFDAC** — the agency that stands between Nigerians and fake drugs.

She took the job. And she found a country where, by the agency's own surveys, something like **four in every ten drugs on sale were counterfeit.** People were dying — her own sister had died after taking fake insulin.

She went to war with it. They burned NAFDAC facilities. They left fetish objects in her office — blood-stained feathers, beads. And in December 2003, near Agulu in Anambra State, gunmen shot at her car and **a bullet went through her headscarf.**

She kept going. By 2005, counterfeit drug prevalence had fallen from around 41% to under 17%.

She died of cancer on 7 June 2014.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: The public test is passed in private, years earlier.**

Everybody remembers the bullet through the headscarf. Almost nobody remembers the twelve thousand pounds. But the twelve thousand pounds came first — and it is the reason she was ever in the room.

Psalm 119:30 says *"I have chosen the way of faithfulness."* Past tense. Already done. By the time the gunmen came, the choosing was long finished. She was not deciding under fire; she was **revealing** under fire what she had already decided in a quiet hotel room in England.

Ask yourself the uncomfortable version of this: what have you already decided, that a crisis would reveal?

**Truth Two: A nation's trust is destroyed one transaction at a time — and rebuilt the same way.**

Fake drugs were not a private sin. Every single fake tablet was one person deciding, in one transaction, that a stranger's life was worth less than their margin. Multiply that by millions and you get a country where a mother cannot trust a chemist.

Which means the reverse is also true. Every honest transaction you make — every correct change, every accurate invoice, every exam you do not cheat — is a brick. Nobody claps. It still counts.

And here is the hard part of Dora Akunyili's story: after she left, the gains did not hold. Fake drugs are back. Which tells you that integrity is not a monument you build once. It is a discipline a nation has to keep choosing, generation by generation. That is why we are teaching this to children.

### 5. THE BUILD

Today, find one transaction where you could quietly take something that is not yours — extra change, an unclaimed hour, a small padding on a figure — **and don't.**

Nobody will know. That is the whole point.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, make me part of the generation that ends the culture of convenient lies — starting with my own. Let me choose the way of faithfulness before the test comes, not during it. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
A woman called Dora was given money for an operation. Then the doctor said, "You don't need the operation!" She could have kept the money and nobody would have known. But she gave it back. Later on, God gave her a very big job protecting Nigerians from bad medicine — and she was brave, even when dangerous people tried to stop her. God trusted her with the big thing because she was honest with the small thing.
*Ask them:* **Has anyone ever given you too much change? What did you do?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Counterfeit medicine kills across Africa, Asia and Latin America. Wherever you live, someone is deciding today whether a stranger's life is worth their margin.

**ACCURACY NOTE:**

- **SAFE —** DG of NAFDAC **April 2001 – December 2008**; Minister of Information 2008–2010; assassination attempt **December 2003**, bullet through her headscarf; died **7 June 2014**; counterfeit prevalence fell from ~41% (2002) to ~16.7% (2005/06) **on NAFDAC's own surveys**; her sister died of fake insulin.
- **CAUTION — The money-back story belongs to the Petroleum Trust Fund, BEFORE NAFDAC — not to NAFDAC.** Saying "she refused bribes at NAFDAC" conflates two things. Tell it as this guide does: she got the NAFDAC job *because* she had already returned money that was hers to keep.
- **CAUTION —** Say "by the agency's own surveys" on the 41%→17% figures. No independent prevalence survey has been published since ~2005, and the gains did not hold — say that too, as this script does. It is honest and it strengthens the point.
- **CAUTION —** Nobody was ever convicted of the shooting. Don't imply justice was done.

# DAY 3

## "The Ones Who Showed Up"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 3 · Integrity & Truth-Seeking*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

I am going to say six names today. Most Nigerians have never heard them. They were young — your age, or your children's age. They finished university, they were posted for national service, and they were given a job: help run an election.

They did the job. And it killed them.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Proverbs 12:22 (NIV):**
*"The Lord detests lying lips, but he delights in people who are trustworthy."*

**Psalm 119:11 (NIV):**
*"I have hidden your word in my heart that I might not sin against you."*

### 3. THE STORY

April 2011. Nigeria held a presidential election on the sixteenth. Across the country, thousands of **NYSC corps members** — young graduates doing their compulsory year of national service — were deployed as election officials. It is ordinary, unglamorous work: sit at a polling unit, count, record, report.

When the results were announced, rioting broke out across twelve northern states. Over the next three days, **more than eight hundred people were killed.**

Corps members were among the targets, because they were identified with the election. **Human Rights Watch documented ten corps members killed in Bauchi State alone**, including two young women.

Six of them died together at the **Divisional Police Headquarters in Giade, Bauchi State.** They had run there for shelter. A mob followed them and burned the station down.

Their names were **Nkwazema Anslem** from Imo, **Adewumi Seun Paul** from Ekiti, **Okpokiri Obinna** from Abia, **Teidi Olawale Tosin** from Kogi, **Adewei Elliot** from Bayelsa, and **Ukeoma Ikechukwu Chibuzor** from Imo.

Look at that list of states. Imo. Ekiti. Abia. Kogi. Bayelsa. They came from all over Nigeria to serve a part of Nigeria that was not their own. That is what NYSC is *for*.

Suspects in that attack were finally arrested and paraded in **May 2024** — thirteen years later.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Integrity is mostly just showing up where you said you would.**

We romanticise integrity. We imagine a dramatic moment where you refuse a bribe with a great speech. Most of the time it is far less cinematic than that. It is **turning up to do a dull job properly when nobody would have blamed you for finding an excuse.**

These six young people did not make a speech. They took a posting, they showed up, they did the work. That is what got them killed, and that is what makes them worth naming.

Proverbs says God *"delights"* in people who are trustworthy. Not admires. Not tolerates. Delights. There is a God who takes pleasure in a person who simply does what they said they would do.

**Truth Two: Where integrity comes from — and when you have to install it.**

Psalm 119:11 is precise about the mechanism: *"I have hidden your word in my heart"* — **so that** — *"I might not sin against you."*

The hiding comes first. Long before.

You cannot download courage on the morning you need it. On the day the mob arrives, whatever is in you is what is in you. That is why we memorise. That is why we teach children Scripture before they can possibly need it. We are not filling time. We are **installing something ahead of the test.**

### 5. THE BUILD

Two things today.

First: **memorise Psalm 119:11.** Actually memorise it. Say it out loud five times before you sleep. You are installing.

Second: if you know a serving corps member — or a teacher, or a nurse, or anyone in a posting far from home — **send them one message today** saying you see them. Nigeria is very bad at thanking the people who simply showed up.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, give me the courage to guard what is true, even when guarding it costs me something. And put Your word in me now, before the day I need it. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
When Nigerians finish university, they spend one year helping a part of Nigeria that is not their home. Some young people were doing that job, helping to count votes in an election. Some very angry people hurt them. They were brave just by doing their job properly. Being good is not always about big speeches — sometimes it is just doing what you promised, even when it is boring, even when it is hard.
*Ask them:* **What is one job you have been given at home that you sometimes don't feel like doing?**

**ACCURACY NOTE — READ THIS ONE CAREFULLY:**

- **DO NOT — The original draft said these corps members were "killed while guarding ballot boxes, refusing to falsify results." There is NO documented evidence for that.** Human Rights Watch and the Nigeria Police both describe corps members *fleeing to police stations for safety* and being pursued there by mobs after the results were announced. There is no documented case of a named 2011 corps member being killed for refusing to alter a result. **Do not say it.** Nigerian fact-checkers and the victims' families would know.
- **SAFE —** election **16 April 2011**; rioting 17–19 April across twelve northern states; **HRW: more than 800 killed**; **HRW: ten corps members killed in Bauchi State**; six died at Giade Divisional Police HQ; the six names above are from the Bauchi State Police Command (May 2024); suspects paraded **30 May 2024**.
- **CAUTION —** Do not say "hundreds of corps members." Numbers vary by source; use HRW's ten in Bauchi and the six at Giade.
- **CAUTION —** Two of the victims were young women who were raped before being killed. **Do not put that on camera.** It is in the record if someone raises it; it does not belong in a family broadcast.
- **CAUTION —** In 2013 an opposition party alleged some of those described as murdered corps members were party agents, not serving corps members. It was never resolved. Naming the six confirmed Giade victims is the safest ground — which is why this script names them.
- **CAUTION — BEYOND NIGERIA** line for this day: every country has people who die doing ordinary public duty — poll workers, election observers, census takers. Name one from your own country if you can.

# DAY 4

## "Trust Is Money"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 4 · Integrity & Truth-Seeking*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

Suppose I told you there is a business system in Nigeria where a young man works for a master for years — sometimes seven years — for no salary. And at the end, the master hands him capital, goods, sometimes a shop, and sometimes even a share of his own customers, and sends him out to trade.

Your first question should be: **why would the master ever do that?**

Hold that question. The answer is the whole lesson today.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Proverbs 24:26 (NIV):**
*"An honest answer is like a kiss on the lips."*

**Psalm 119:160 (NIV):**
*"All your words are true; all your righteous laws are eternal."*

### 3. THE STORY

Among the Igbo of south-eastern Nigeria there is a system called **Igba Boi** — "to serve as a boy." A young man, a *nwa boi*, apprentices himself to a trader. He serves. He learns the business from the inside. And at the end of an agreed term — historically five to eight years — the master **"settles"** him: startup capital, goods, sometimes premises.

That young man then becomes a master himself, and settles his own apprentices. And so it multiplies. In Alaba International Market in Lagos alone, the American journalist Robert Neuwirth counted roughly **ten thousand merchants doing something like four billion dollars of trade a year** — and called it one of the largest business incubators in the world.

So — back to the question. Why does the master settle the boy? He signed nothing. There was no contract. No court would have enforced it.

Here is the answer, and it is not the romantic one. He settles him **because his own community would destroy him if he didn't.** Kinship ties. Town unions. The guarantors who vouched for the boy in the first place. A man who cheats his apprentice in Nnewi cannot show his face in Nnewi.

The system did not run on warm feelings. It ran on **accountability that everybody could see.**

And where that accountability was weak, the system failed people. Boys taken as children and separated from their parents. Masters who took seven years of labour and then found a reason not to settle. Girls almost entirely excluded. That is why, in September 2025, Anambra State passed the **Igbo Apprenticeship Law** — capping the term at seven years, requiring a written contract, requiring a minimum level of schooling, and making settlement a legally enforceable obligation.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Integrity is economic infrastructure.**

Where a person's word is reliable, capital moves. Where it isn't, capital hides — and a country can sit on an ocean of oil and stay poor. That is not a sermon; that is an accounting fact. Every contract you cannot trust has to be replaced with a lawyer, a deposit, a guarantee, an escrow — and all of that is cost. **Corruption is not just wicked. It is expensive.**

Psalm 119:160 says *"all your righteous laws are eternal."* The naira has lost enormous value in our lifetime. Truthfulness has not lost any. It is the one currency that never inflates away.

**Truth Two: Trust needs a structure to stand in.**

This is the correction we have to make, and it is the more useful lesson. People love to say the apprenticeship system runs "on nothing but trust." It doesn't. It runs on trust *plus* guarantors, *plus* town unions, *plus* public reputation, and now *plus* a state law.

Do not read that as cynicism. Read it as instruction. If you want an honest organisation — a church, a business, a school, a household — **do not just hope for honest people. Build the structure that makes honesty visible.** Who checks the money? Who sees the books? Who can raise a concern without being punished?

Trust is a plant. Accountability is the trellis. Take away the trellis and the plant does not become more free. It falls over.

### 5. THE BUILD

Look at one thing you are responsible for — money, a project, a team, a household budget. Ask: **"Who would know if I did this wrong?"**

If the answer is "nobody," you have found today's assignment. Go and tell one trustworthy person what you are doing and ask them to check on you. That is not weakness. That is a trellis.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, let my word become currency — something people can build on because it never fails them. And give me the humility to be accountable, not just to be trusted. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
In some parts of Nigeria, a young man goes to work for a shop owner for many years without getting paid. Then at the end, the shop owner gives him money and goods so he can start his own shop! Why does the shop owner keep his promise? Because the whole village is watching, and nobody wants to be known as a person who breaks promises. Being trustworthy is worth more than money — because people will only give you money if they trust you.
*Ask them:* **Who is someone you completely trust? What makes you trust them?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Every economy runs on trust it cannot see. Ask what holds honesty in place where you live — and whether it is getting stronger or weaker.

**ACCURACY NOTE:**

- **DO NOT — Do NOT say "Harvard Business School studied the Igba Boi system."** This is repeated constantly in Nigerian media and it is wrong. What exists is an article by Ndubuisi Ekekwe in **Harvard Business Review** (19 May 2021). HBR is a magazine; it is not an HBS case study or curriculum. Say "Harvard Business Review published an article on it."
- **DO NOT — "Largest business incubator in the world" is not Harvard's phrase.** It comes from the American journalist **Robert Neuwirth**, in a 2018 TED talk about Alaba International Market. Attribute it to him, as this script does.
- **CAUTION —** Don't say "built on nothing but trust." It is not true, and the correction is a better lesson — which is why it is Truth Two.
- **CAUTION —** Do not quote national scale figures ("X million businesses," "N-trillion settled"). No such survey exists. Neuwirth's Alaba numbers (~10,000 merchants, ~$4bn/year) are the only concrete sourced figures.
- **CAUTION —** Don't tie the system's origin to the civil war. It predates Biafra by centuries; the BBC framing that made this popular has been publicly disputed, including by Ekekwe himself.
- **SAFE —** **Anambra State Igbo Apprenticeship Law, 2025**, effective 10 September 2025 — seven-year cap, written contracts, minimum JSS3 education, enforceable settlement, and an Igbo Apprenticeship Commission.

# PART TWO: DISCIPLINE & DILIGENCE

# DAY 5

## "The Ten-Year Bet"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 5 · Discipline & Diligence*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

I want to be careful today, because I am going to talk about Africa's richest man, and the story you have probably been told about him is not quite true.

He did not start from nothing. And once you know that, the *real* lesson in his life gets much more useful to you — because the real lesson is not about how he started. It is about what he did for ten years when almost nobody believed him.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Ecclesiastes 9:10 (NIV):**
*"Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with all your might."*

**Psalm 119:32 (NIV):**
*"I run in the path of your commands, for you have broadened my understanding."*

### 3. THE STORY

**Aliko Dangote** was born in Kano in 1957. In 1977, at about twenty years old, he started trading — sugar, rice, salt, cement — with a large loan from his family, which he repaid within three months.

Now let us be honest about that family. His great-grandfather, Alhassan Dantata, was reputed to be the richest man in West Africa when he died. His grandfather, Sanusi Dantata, was once Kano's wealthiest man. Aliko Dangote did not arrive in Lagos with nothing. When a Nigerian governor said something like that publicly in 2023, Nigerian fact-checkers publicly corrected him.

So where is the lesson?

Here. In 2013, Dangote announced he was going to build a petroleum refinery in Nigeria. Not import fuel — *refine* it. In a country that had spent decades failing to make its own refineries work.

People laughed. Analysts said it could not be financed. The site at Lekki was swamp; they had to pump in sand for years before they could build. The cost ran past nineteen billion dollars.

It was commissioned in **May 2023**. It produced diesel and aviation fuel from **January 2024**, petrol from **September 2024**, and reached its full design capacity of **650,000 barrels a day in February 2026.**

Ten years. Ten years between the announcement and the thing actually working at full capacity. Ten years of being the man who said he would do the impossible thing and had not yet done it.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Discipline is what you do in the middle, when the applause has stopped and the finish is not in sight.**

Anybody can be excited at an announcement. Anybody can celebrate at a ribbon-cutting. **Year six is where character lives** — when the swamp is still swamp, the money is late, and the people who laughed at you have moved on to laughing at somebody else.

Ecclesiastes says *"do it with all your might."* Not "with all your enthusiasm." Might is what you use when enthusiasm has run out.

**Truth Two: Obedience is not a cage. It is a road.**

Psalm 119:32 says *"I run in the path of your commands, for you have broadened my understanding."* Two things there. First: he **runs**. He is not trudging. Second: the reason he can run is that God has *broadened* something in him — given him room.

We tend to think of discipline as narrowing: fewer options, less freedom. Scripture keeps saying the opposite. The person who cannot get up in the morning is not free. The person who cannot finish anything is not free. Discipline is what gives you enough width to run.

### 5. THE BUILD

Name one thing you started and abandoned. One. Write down the exact reason you stopped.

Now decide: **am I restarting this, or am I honestly letting it go?** Either answer is fine. Drifting is not. Nation-builders do not have a graveyard of half-finished things; they have a short list of finished ones.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, give me hands that keep working long after the feeling that started the work has gone. Broaden my understanding, so that obedience feels like running and not like carrying. Amen."*

Ten years of digging and building while people said it would never work. But he did not stop. And now it works. Some good things take a very, very long time. That does not mean they are not going to happen.

Thirty years ago, a Nigerian woman jumped seven metres and twelve centimetres in Atlanta, Georgia, and won this country's first Olympic gold medal. It is still the only individual Olympic gold Nigeria has ever won.

She has always insisted she never knowingly took anything to enhance her performance — she has said, *"I never one day go to the chemist to buy something to take to enhance my performance"* — and she attributed the result to medication she had been prescribed in Nigeria. Her appeals were rejected.

Four years. The Barcelona Olympics came and went without her. During most of that ban, by her own account and by reporting since, she did not train for long jump at all.

The ban expired shortly before the 1996 Atlanta Games. She had barely trained for six months. She got no meaningful support from Nigeria — she has said so directly: *"I worked very hard, personally, not with the help of Nigeria, but with the help of God."*

On **2 August 1996**, on her **first attempt**, she jumped **7.12 metres**. An African record. Gold.

It was Nigeria's first Olympic gold medal of any kind. It was the first Olympic gold won by a Black African woman in a field event. And thirty years later, it is still Nigeria's only individual Olympic gold.

She went on to campaign publicly against doping and to found a foundation, in December 2017, to develop young Nigerian athletes. She is today an Assistant Commissioner of Police.

We would rather tell you a clean story. Scripture almost never does. Moses had killed a man. David had a scandal that would end a career today. Peter denied Christ out loud, three times, and then preached the sermon that started the church.

The Bible is not a collection of unblemished people. It is a collection of **restored** ones.

So hear this properly: Chioma Ajunwa lost four years of her athletic prime. Whatever you believe about how it happened, those four years were gone. And she came back and did something no Nigerian has done before or since.

If you have a record — a failure, a public one, something people still bring up — this day is for you. **The four lost years were not the end of her story. They were the middle of it.**

**Truth Two: Discipline that lasts is discipline that has been formally committed to.**

Psalm 119:106 says *"I have taken an oath and confirmed it."* Not "I hope to." Not "I intend to." He made a vow, and then he confirmed it — went back and said it again.

Feelings will not carry you through four years in the wilderness. A decision might. Paul says the athlete *"goes into strict training"* — a settled arrangement, not a mood.

And notice what Paul says the athlete is training for: *"a crown that will not last."* He is not mocking athletes. He is saying: look how hard people work for a medal that tarnishes. **Now look at what we are running for.**

### 5. THE BUILD

Make one small formal commitment today — and confirm it to another human being out loud, so it is on the record.

Not "I'll try to read my Bible more." Something you could be held to: *"I will read one Psalm every morning in October, and I am telling you so you can ask me."*

That is Psalm 119:106 in practice: taken, and confirmed.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, train me in the unseen hours, so I am ready when the visible moment comes. And where I have lost years, redeem them. Amen."*

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Every nation has athletes who came back from a ban, a break, or an injury. Restoration is a universal story — and a deeply biblical one.

### 1. THE HOOK

Yesterday we talked about a decade. Today let us talk about the years before anybody is watching at all — the apprentice years, the errand years, the years where the work is small and repetitive and nobody would notice if you did it badly.

Those years are not the waiting room. They are the training.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Proverbs 10:4 (NIV):**
*"Lazy hands make for poverty, but diligent hands bring wealth."*

**Psalm 119:60 (NIV):**
*"I will hasten and not delay to obey your commands."*

### 3. THE STORY

**Innocent Chukwuma** was born in 1961. He served a traditional Nnewi apprenticeship — *igba boi*, the system we looked at on Day 4 — under a dealer in motorcycle spare parts.

Spare parts. Not vehicles. Parts.

He traded with his brother, and then in 1981 began trading Honda parts under his own name: **Innoson Nigeria Limited.**

From parts, he moved to assembling motorcycles. From motorcycles, to buses. And in 2007 he established **Innoson Vehicle Manufacturing** — the first wholly indigenous, privately-owned vehicle manufacturer in Nigeria. The plant at Nnewi was commissioned by President Goodluck Jonathan on **15 October 2010.**

Look at the ladder. Spare parts. Then assembling. Then buses. Then a factory. Nobody handed him a factory. He built a staircase out of increasingly larger versions of the same competence.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: No empire begins at the top.**

Every large thing you admire was, at some point, somebody doing a small thing well for a long time with nobody watching. Proverbs 10:4 sets it up as a straight contrast — lazy hands, diligent hands — and it locates the difference in the **hands**, not in the circumstances.

That is not a promise that diligence always produces wealth. Proverbs is describing how the world generally works, not issuing a guarantee. But the direction is unmistakable: **the person who does small work carelessly is not building toward anything.**

If you are currently doing something small — an apprenticeship, a first job, a subject you find boring, a market stall — do not treat it as the delay before your real life. It is the material your real life will be made of.

**Truth Two: Diligence includes speed.**

Psalm 119:60 is a verse people skip: *"I will hasten and not delay."* We usually think of diligence as *working hard*. The psalmist adds something: **not postponing.**

There is a kind of laziness that looks extremely busy. It works hard on everything except the one thing it is avoiding. You know exactly what yours is.

Diligence is doing the work. Hastening is doing **the right work, today**, especially the one you have been putting off.

### 5. THE BUILD

Identify the one task you have been carrying for more than a week that would take under thirty minutes.

Do it before you sleep tonight. That is the whole assignment. *Hasten and not delay.*

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, let me be as faithful in the small, unseen tasks as I hope to be trusted with the large, visible ones. And where I have been delaying, give me the courage to simply start. Amen."*

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**

And then he built a whole factory that makes cars in Nigeria! He did not start with a factory. He started with a bolt.
*Ask them:* **What small job do you have? How could you do it a little bit better today?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Almost every manufacturing nation began with someone assembling other people's parts. The ladder from trading to making is the same in Korea, in India, in Nigeria.

# DAY 8

## "Ten Thousand Villages"

**RUNTIME:** ~7 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 8 · Discipline & Diligence*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

There is a Nigerian achievement that almost nobody celebrates, because it happened slowly, over nearly a quarter of a century, and it was carried out mostly by women you have never heard of, walking.

It is arguably the greatest thing this country has done in my lifetime. And it was made entirely out of discipline.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — 1 Corinthians 9:27 (NIV):**
*"No, I strike a blow to my body and make it my slave so that after I have preached to others, I myself will not be disqualified for the prize."*

**Psalm 119:112 (NIV):**
*"My heart is set on keeping your decrees to the very end."*

### 3. THE STORY

Polio is a virus that paralyses children. For most of the twentieth century it was everywhere. And by the 2000s, Nigeria was one of the last places on earth where **wild poliovirus** was still circulating — at one point accounting for the majority of the world's cases.

Stopping it required something almost absurdly unglamorous: finding **every single child**, in every settlement, in every state, and putting two drops in their mouth. Then doing it again. Then again. Round after round, year after year.

Vaccinators walked. They crossed rivers. They went into settlements with no roads and no addresses. They went back to the same compound four times because a mother had said no three times.

It was dangerous. On the morning of **8 February 2013**, gunmen attacked two polio clinics in Kano where vaccinators were assembling, killing at least nine of them. It was part of a wave of attacks that month in which **thirteen health workers were killed across Kano and Borno.** One immunisation round was suspended and the follow-up cancelled.

And then the campaigns continued.

Nigeria's last cases of wild poliovirus had their onset in **August 2016**, in Borno State — the hardest place in the country to reach. Four years later, on **25 August 2020**, the **WHO African Region was certified free of wild poliovirus** — a milestone that hinged entirely on Nigeria, the last African country where the wild virus was still circulating.

Nobody built a monument. There was no gold medal. Just a virus that used to paralyse Nigerian children, and now does not.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: The greatest work is usually repetitive, unglamorous, and long.**

We want our contribution to be dramatic. God's work in a nation is mostly **the same faithful act, repeated, until something structural changes.**

Two drops. Next house. Two drops. Next house. From the first national immunisation days in 1996 to certification in 2020.

Psalm 119:112 says *"My heart is set on keeping your decrees — to the very end."* That last phrase is the whole verse. Anyone can start. Anyone can be enthusiastic in round one. The vaccinators of round forty-seven, in the rain, in a settlement that shouted at them last time — those are the people who ended wild polio in Nigeria.

**Truth Two: Discipline is active resistance, not passive good intention.**

Paul's language in 1 Corinthians 9:27 is violent: *"I strike a blow to my body and make it my slave."* He is not describing gentle self-improvement. He is describing a fight.

Because the body — and the will, and the attention — drifts toward ease automatically. You do not have to *decide* to be undisciplined; it is the default. Discipline is the thing you have to keep choosing, against a current that never switches off.

And notice Paul's reason: *"so that after I have preached to others, I myself will not be disqualified."* He is terrified of being a man whose message outran his life. That fear is healthy. Keep it.

### 5. THE BUILD

Pick one thing you will do **every single day for the rest of October** — small enough that you cannot fail. Five minutes of Scripture. One press-up. One page. One call to your mother.

Small enough that you cannot fail, and then do not fail. Twenty-two days from now, we will ask.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, train my discipline to outlast my motivation. Set my heart on keeping Your word — to the very end, not just at the beginning. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Smallpox, polio, guinea worm — every disease humanity has ever pushed back was pushed back by repetition, not by genius.

# DAY 9

## "The Furnace That Was Never Lit"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 9 · Wisdom & Discernment*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

Nigeria has a steel plant that is ninety-eight per cent finished. It has been ninety-eight per cent finished for about thirty years.

Forty of its forty-three units are built. Guess which three are not.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Proverbs 24:3 (NIV):**
*"By wisdom a house is built, and through understanding it is established."*

**Psalm 119:98 (NIV):**
*"Your commands are always with me and make me wiser than my enemies."*

### 3. THE STORY

Construction of the **Ajaokuta Steel Company** began in **1979**, under President Shehu Shagari, built by a Soviet contractor called Tiazhpromexport. The vision was real and it was right: a country that makes its own steel does not have to import its own industrial future.

It was never commissioned. Not once. In forty-seven years it has never produced a single tonne of steel from its own furnaces.

Now — which three units of the forty-three were never finished? **The blast furnace. The coke oven. The steel-making shop.** In other words: all the parts that actually make steel.

But even that is not the deepest problem. Ajaokuta's blast furnace was designed to run on iron ore from Itakpe — and raw Itakpe ore has too little iron in it to use directly. It has to be processed first, at a beneficiation plant. **That plant has never worked properly.** And the fifty-two-kilometre railway line meant to carry the ore from Itakpe to Ajaokuta was never completed.

You cannot light a blast furnace without a guaranteed, continuous supply of feedstock. So it has never been lit.

Meanwhile: a *Saturday Punch* investigation of the company's own budgets found that between 2014 and 2024, the Federal Government paid **₦38.9 billion in salaries and allowances** at Ajaokuta — for zero output. A senator who visited repeatedly said that on her visits, "hardly were 10 people sighted to be around or doing anything."

And in July 2026, Nigeria's Minister of Steel Development said Ajaokuta needs one and a half to two billion dollars and that a deal with a Chinese company was being targeted "before the end of this year."

Forty-seven years of such announcements is itself part of the story.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Wisdom is seeing the whole system, not the impressive part.**

Ajaokuta did not fail because somebody was stupid. It failed because a nation built the **magnificent visible thing** — the plant — without building the boring invisible things it completely depended on: the ore processing, and the railway.

That is one of the most common failures in human life, and it is not only a government failure. It is the business with a beautiful shopfront and no bookkeeping. The ministry with a big vision and no volunteers trained. The household with a school fee plan and no emergency fund. **The marriage with a wedding and no conversations.**

Proverbs 24:3 is precise: *"By wisdom a house is built, and through understanding it is established."* Two different words. Wisdom gets it built. **Understanding keeps it standing.** Nigeria has never had a shortage of vision. We have had a chronic shortage of the second thing.

**Truth Two: Wisdom is a source you consult, not a quality you possess.**

Psalm 119:98 does not say "I am wiser than my enemies." It says *"Your commands... make me wiser."* The wisdom is not native. It is **accessed**.

Which means it is available to you — you do not have to be born clever. And it means it does you no good unless you actually go and get it. A source you never consult makes you no wiser than having no source at all.

Practically, for a builder: before the next thing you start, go and find the person who has already failed at it. Ask them what broke. That conversation is worth more than the launch.

### 5. THE BUILD

Take one thing you are currently building — a business, a ministry, a course, a home.

Ask: **"What is the boring, unglamorous thing this depends on that I have not built?"** The bookkeeping. The maintenance plan. The second person who knows how it works.

Write it down. Start it this week. That is your railway line.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, give me the humility to ask before I build, and the discernment to listen even when the answer is not the one I wanted. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
Nigeria built a giant factory to make steel. It is almost finished — but the parts that actually make the steel were never built, and the train line to bring in the rocks was never finished either. So the whole huge factory has never made anything. It is like building a beautiful kitchen with no cooker and no way to buy food. When we build something, we must think about *everything* it needs — not just the exciting parts.
*Ask them:* **If you were going to bake a cake, what is one thing you'd need that nobody thinks about?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Every country has a stranded megaproject. The pattern — visible asset, missing supporting system — is universal.

**ACCURACY NOTE:**

- **CAUTION — Say "begun in 1979," NOT "commissioned in 1979."** In engineering, "commissioned" means *brought into operation* — and the entire point is that Ajaokuta never was. Engineers in your audience will correct you.
- **CAUTION — "Never produced commercial steel" is right; "never produced anything at all" is wrong.** Ajaokuta's *rolling mills* have intermittently rolled products using **imported billets from Ukraine**, and light mills operated in 2018 for small iron-rod fabrication. Precise wording: *"never produced a single tonne of steel from its own furnaces. What it has rolled, it rolled from steel imported from abroad."*
- **CAUTION — Avoid the "$8–10 billion" figure** unless you attribute it as an estimate — it circulates widely but is not audited. Harder numbers to use instead: **₦38.9bn in salaries 2014–2024 for zero output** (Saturday Punch investigation), and **$496 million paid in 2022** to settle claims by Global Infrastructure Holdings after the 2004 concession was terminated in 2008.
- **DO NOT — Do not claim "ignored technical warnings."** No specific documented warning-and-override is on record (see Day 23's note). The sequencing failure — plant without beneficiation plant without railway — *is* documented, and is a sharper lesson anyway.
- **CAUTION —** Do not present the revival as accomplished. As of August 2026 nothing has been concluded.

*"I have more insight than all my teachers, for I meditate on your statutes."*

### 3. THE STORY

On **17 January 1955**, the Western Region of Nigeria launched **free universal primary education.** The Premier was **Chief Obafemi Awolowo**; the scheme had been proposed to the House of Assembly in July 1952 by his Education Minister, Chief S.O. Awokoya.

Enrolment went from **456,600 fee-paying pupils in 1954 to 811,432 in 1955.** Teacher numbers went from about 17,000 to about 27,000. Capital spending on school buildings was £2.4 million in 1955 alone.

Now — where did the money come from?

From cocoa. And specifically, from the **Cocoa Marketing Board**, which bought cocoa from farmers at below world prices and kept the surplus. Plus a regional education levy.

Say that plainly, because it matters: **peasant cocoa farmers were effectively taxed to educate the region's children.** Not investors. Not oil. Farmers, many of them poor, many of whose own children were among the first to walk into those classrooms.

There was opposition. There was real fiscal strain. The planners had projected 160,000 new pupils and got more than double that; classrooms were improvised and many teachers were untrained.

And there is a comparison that makes the lesson sharper. The Eastern Region launched its own universal primary education scheme in **February 1957**, partly in political competition with the West. Within a year it hit a financial crisis and had to scale the scheme back.

The difference was not ambition. Both regions were ambitious. **The West had a dedicated, ring-fenced revenue stream and four years of planning. The East announced first and looked for the money afterwards.**

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Wisdom builds for the harvest, not the applause.**

A school opening is a boring photograph. A monument is a beautiful one. Proverbs 10:14 says the wise **"store up"** knowledge — it is a verb about accumulation over time, about something that pays out later.

Nigeria's political culture rewards the ribbon-cutting. Wisdom is the discipline of investing in things whose returns arrive after you have left office — after you have left the house, the company, the church. **Plant trees you will not sit under.**

And notice the cost was real. It was not free money. Somebody's cocoa paid for it. Every genuine investment in a nation's future is paid for by somebody in the present — usually somebody who does not get the credit.

**Truth Two: Insight comes from reflection, not just information.**

Psalm 119:99 is a startling claim: *"I have more insight than all my teachers."* Why? *"For I meditate on your statutes."*

He is not saying he knows more facts than his teachers. He is saying he has done something with what he received: sat with it, turned it over, applied it.

Nigeria is not short of information. We have degrees, conferences, consultants, reports. What the East lacked in 1957 was not information about how to run a school system — it was the reflective discipline of asking *"and where exactly will the money come from in year three?"*

Ask that question about your own plans. It is a very cheap question and it saves very expensive mistakes.

### 5. THE BUILD

Think of one commitment you have made — financial, ministry, family — that you announced before you fully funded.

Today, do the boring arithmetic. Write down where the money, or the time, or the people are actually coming from in month six.

If the answer is "we'll see," you have just done the most useful thing anyone will do all day.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, give me the patience to build for the harvest and not for the applause. And teach me to reflect on what I already know before I ask for more. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
Seventy years ago, a leader in Nigeria decided that every child should be able to go to school for free. Suddenly there were hundreds of thousands of new children in classrooms! But somebody had to pay — and it was the cocoa farmers, working in the sun, whose money paid for the schools. Somebody always pays for good things. It is worth saying thank you.
*Ask them:* **Who pays for you to go to school? Have you thanked them?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Universal primary education was won the same way everywhere — by one generation paying for the next one's classrooms.

# DAY 11

## "The Words and the Follow-Through"

**RUNTIME:** ~7 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 11 · Wisdom & Discernment*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

Today is a difficult day, and I want to teach it honestly, because a lot of people watching this carry a wound about it in their family.

On the day Nigeria's civil war ended, the man who had won said something remarkable. And then the years that followed did not entirely match it. Both of those things are true, and wisdom means being able to hold both.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — James 1:5 (NIV):**
*"If any of you lacks wisdom, you should ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault, and it will be given to you."*

**Psalm 119:100 (NIV):**
*"I have more understanding than the elders, for I obey your precepts."*

### 3. THE STORY

The Nigerian Civil War ran from 1967 to 1970 and killed, by most estimates, over a million people, a great many of them children who starved.

On **12 January 1970**, Lt-Col Philip Effiong, who had become acting Biafran head of state, announced the surrender. On **15 January 1970**, the formal instrument was signed at Dodan Barracks in Lagos.

That same day, the Head of State, **General Yakubu Gowon**, broadcast to the nation and proclaimed what he called the dawn of national reconciliation. The phrase that has lasted is **"no victor, no vanquished."** It came with a policy remembered as the **3Rs**: Reconciliation, Rehabilitation, Reconstruction.

And by the standards of civil wars anywhere in the world, what did *not* happen next is extraordinary. There were **no mass war-crimes trials.** No mass executions of the losing side. A general amnesty. Philip Effiong himself was briefly detained and then released.

That mattered. Enormously. Ask anyone who has studied how civil wars usually end.

But this is where honesty is required. Because in the years that followed, a set of policies landed very hard on the Igbo. Holders of Biafran currency who brought it in received a flat **£20 payment**, whatever the sum they tendered. There was an "abandoned property" policy in Port Harcourt and Rivers under which Igbo property was not returned. And in 1972 the Nigerian Enterprises Promotion Decree transferred foreign-owned businesses into Nigerian hands — at a moment when, many historians argue, Igbo families had no capital left to participate.

Gowon himself, writing in his memoir and quoted in Nigerian press as recently as August 2026, disputes part of this. He says the £20 applied to **Biafran currency** brought back for exchange, and that anyone who could prove they had money in a **Nigerian** bank got it back in full with interest. Others, including Chinua Achebe, described it very differently.

The historians are still arguing. And Nigerians are still arguing.

So here is the honest summary, and it is the one I would say on camera: **He said the right thing, and saying it stopped the killing. Whether the policies that followed matched the words is a question this country has never finished answering.**

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Wisdom at a national level means choosing what heals over what satisfies.**

In January 1970, Gowon had the power to crush. Every incentive of victory pointed toward humiliation, trials, retribution. He chose language of reconciliation instead — and a million more people did not die.

That is what wisdom looks like when you are holding power. It is not soft. It is the hardest thing available. And James 1:5 says it is the one thing God has promised to give generously to anyone who asks — *"without finding fault."* God does not lecture you for not already having it. He gives it.

**Truth Two: Reconciliation is not a sentence. It is a settlement.**

And this is the second truth, and it is for us, not for Gowon.

Words of reconciliation are necessary and they are not sufficient. If you say "I forgive you" and then quietly keep the person's property, you have not reconciled — you have performed reconciliation. Real reconciliation costs the stronger party something concrete.

Psalm 119:100 says *"I have more understanding than the elders, for I obey your precepts."* Understanding is tied to **obedience**, not to age, rank, or position. Which is a striking thing for a young person to be told: you can understand a situation more truly than someone twice your age and ten times your title, if you are actually doing what God says and they are not.

So the question for a nation-builder is never just *did we say the right words?* It is *did we do the settlement?* In your family, in your church, in your business — where have you said the word and skipped the settlement?

### 5. THE BUILD

Think of one relationship where words of peace have been said but something concrete was never returned or repaired. Money. An apology to a third party. A recognition never given publicly.

Do the concrete part this week. That is the difference between saying reconciliation and doing it.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, when I have the power to crush, give me the wisdom to choose what heals. And do not let me stop at the words. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
Nigeria once had a terrible war where Nigerians fought each other. When it ended, the leader said something very important: "There is no winner and no loser — we are all Nigerians." And because of that, the fighting really did stop. But some families still felt they were treated unfairly afterwards. Saying sorry is important. Making things right is important too — and it is harder.
*Ask them:* **If you broke something of your brother's or sister's, is saying sorry enough? What else should you do?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Every post-conflict nation faces the same question — Rwanda, South Africa, Northern Ireland, the American South. Words are the beginning of reconciliation, never the end of it.

- **SAFE —** surrender announced by **Philip Effiong, 12 January 1970**; formal signing at **Dodan Barracks, 15 January 1970**; Gowon proclaimed "the dawn of national reconciliation" that day; the phrase **"no victor, no vanquished"**; the **3Rs**; no mass war-crimes trials; general amnesty; Effiong briefly detained then released. **Gowon is alive** — born 19 October 1934, turned 91 in October 2025; a December 2025 death rumour was formally denied by his aide Adeyeye Ajayi.

- **CAUTION — The Indigenisation Decree argument is historiography, not settled fact.** Say "many historians argue," as this script does. Other accounts hold that the decree ultimately enabled Igbo commercial resurgence.

The sentence "He said the right thing, and whether the follow-through matched is a question we have never finished answering" is both true and the kindest available framing.

# DAY 12

## "The People Who Come First"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 12 · Service & Compassion*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

When the flood comes to a Nigerian city, who arrives first?

Not the agency. Not the NGO. Not, usually, the government. The people already standing in the water.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Galatians 6:2 (NIV):**
*"Carry each other's burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ."*

**Psalm 119:36 (NIV):**
*"Turn my heart toward your statutes and not toward selfish gain."*

### 3. THE STORY

A researcher named Dr Gbenga Shadare spent 2023 and 2024 studying forty-five informal workers in Makoko, Oshodi and Lagos Island — traders, artisans, market people. **Informal workers are 76.3% of the Lagos workforce.** These are not wealthy people. Most of them are one bad week from crisis.

He documented what they did when the floods came.

A market chairman he calls Alhaji Musa told him: **"We organised our members to clean drains and pump water. The government did not come for three days."**

Three days. And they did not wait.

He also documented something quieter and, to me, more remarkable. Market traders run rotating savings groups — *ajo*, *esusu* — where members contribute and take turns receiving the pot. When a trader's stock was destroyed by flood water, **the group paid out early, out of turn**, so she could restock within days.

That is not charity. That is a poor community running its own insurance scheme, and choosing, in the moment, to break its own rules for the member who was drowning.

In Maiduguri in September 2024, when the Alau Dam flood came, the state emergency agency reported over three and a half thousand trapped people rescued. Alongside the official effort, journalists documented "local divers" and "local volunteers" in the water. One man, Babakura Bukar, described how some young men of the area pulled his wife and baby out of a car stuck in the floodwater.

Borno State, we were told, did not have a single boat.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Compassion usually arrives from below.**

It is frequently the person with the least social standing who moves first. Not because the poor are morally superior — they are not — but because **they are already there**, and because they know exactly what it costs to be the one in the water.

Galatians 6:2 says *"carry each other's burdens."* Not "fund a burden-carrying initiative." Carry. It is a verb about your own back.

And here is the thing that should unsettle us: if the first responders in Nigeria are consistently the poorest people present, then the rest of us — with cars, phones, savings, influence — are consistently arriving late. That is the confrontation in today's Scripture, and I am not going to soften it.

**Truth Two: The real obstacle to service is named, and it is not busyness.**

Psalm 119:36 is one of the most honest prayers in Scripture: *"Turn my heart toward your statutes and not toward selfish gain."*

Notice he does not pray "make me less busy." He does not pray "give me more resources." He identifies the actual competitor: **selfish gain.** And he asks God to *turn* his heart — because he knows he cannot simply decide to want different things.

That is a prayer you can pray honestly even when you do not feel generous. *Lord, I do not currently want to help. Turn my heart.*

**And one more thing, because it matters.** Praising informal rescuers without equipping them is hollow. In October 2022 in Ogbaru, Anambra State, an overloaded local boat carrying people fleeing floods capsized and around seventy-six people are feared to have drowned. Untrained, unequipped rescue is not costless — sometimes it kills the people it means to save.

**The poor show up first because nobody else does. That is an indictment of the rest of us, not a substitute for us.**

### 5. THE BUILD

Two options — pick one and do it this week.

**Near:** Find out who in your street, compound or church is one bad week from crisis. Not by asking around — by paying attention. Then do one concrete thing.

**Structural:** Find one group in your area that already responds — a local volunteer team, a church emergency fund, a community savings group — and either join it or resource it. Do not start a new thing. Strengthen the thing that is already there.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, let me see the person in front of me the way You see them — and move toward them, not past them. Turn my heart. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
When floods come to Lagos, guess who helps first? Not always the rich people or the government. Often it is the market traders and the motorcycle riders — people who don't have much money themselves — because they are already there and they just start helping. And market women have a special savings club: when one of them loses everything in a flood, the others give her the money early so she can start selling again.
*Ask them:* **Who is someone at your school who might need help? What could you do this week?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** In every disaster, anywhere on earth, neighbours arrive before agencies. Find the ones near you.

# DAY 13

## "The Doctor Who Said No"

**RUNTIME:** ~7 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 13 · Service & Compassion*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

In 2014, Ebola came to Lagos — a city of over twenty million people, with an international airport, dense markets, and shared transport.

By any reasonable projection, it should have been a catastrophe. Nigeria had twenty cases and eight deaths, and was declared free of the virus in forty-two days.

That did not happen because we were lucky.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — John 15:13 (NIV):**
*"Greater love has no one than this: to lay down one's life for one's friends."*

**Psalm 119:88 (NIV):**
*"In your unfailing love preserve my life, that I may obey the statutes of your mouth."*

### 3. THE STORY

On the evening of **Sunday 20 July 2014**, a Liberian-American finance official named **Patrick Sawyer** collapsed at Murtala Muhammed Airport in Lagos. He was taken to **First Consultants Medical Centre in Obalende**, where a physician named **Dr Ameyo Stella Adadevoh** had worked for twenty-one years.

He was treated at first for malaria. The next day he got worse — vomiting, diarrhoea, bleeding. Dr Adadevoh raised the alarm: she suspected **Ebola**, convened the hospital's incident committee, ordered barrier nursing, and **refused to discharge him** until he was tested.

Then the pressure started.

According to the hospital's medical director, Dr Benjamin Ohiaeri, the **Liberian ambassador to Nigeria telephoned repeatedly**, demanding Sawyer's release, alleging kidnapping and abuse of human rights, and threatening a lawsuit and an international diplomatic row.

Sawyer himself became violent — pulling out his IV line, spilling blood in the room.

She did not let him leave.

Ebola was laboratory-confirmed on **23 July 2014**. Sawyer died on 25 July.

Dr Adadevoh tested positive on **4 August** and died on **19 August 2014**, aged 57. Three of her colleagues died too, including a nurse named **Justina Ejelonu**. Another doctor, **Ada Igonoh**, was infected and survived.

Nigeria's outbreak totalled **20 cases and 8 deaths.** Eight hundred and ninety-four contacts were traced. **The WHO declared Nigeria free of Ebola transmission on 20 October 2014.**

And here is the part that usually gets left out, and should not be. Nigeria contained Ebola because Dr Adadevoh's decision bought time — **and because there was already a polio emergency operations centre in Lagos that was repurposed within days**, and hundreds of contact tracers who made something like eighteen thousand face-to-face visits.

One person's integrity bought the time. A prepared system then used it.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Service at its highest absorbs cost so that others are protected.**

There is generosity that costs you a comfortable margin, and there is service that costs you everything. John 15:13 names the second one and calls it the greatest love there is.

But notice: she did not go looking for martyrdom. She did her job, correctly, and refused to be bullied out of doing it correctly. **The cost came to her; she did not chase it.** That is what most sacrificial service actually looks like — not heroics, but refusing to move when moving would have been easier.

**Truth Two: Individual courage needs a system to land in — and building the system is also service.**

Psalm 119:88 is a prayer to be preserved *"that I may obey the statutes of your mouth."* Life is asked for so that obedience can continue. It is not a prayer for comfort; it is a prayer for more runway to serve.

And this is the practical, unromantic point for nation-builders: **it is not enough to raise heroic individuals. Somebody has to build the boring system.** The emergency centre. The contact tracing protocol. The training. The register. The drill.

If you want to honour Dr Adadevoh, do not just admire her. **Go and build something that means the next Nigerian in her position is not standing alone.** That is service too — and there is far less applause in it.

### 5. THE BUILD

Ask this about one place you have responsibility — your home, your team, your church, your school:

**"If the person who currently holds this together disappeared tomorrow, what would collapse?"**

Fix one of those things this month. Write down the process. Train a second person. That is what makes courage survivable.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, give me the courage to protect others even when it costs me comfort, approval, or safety. And give me the patience to build the systems that let other people be brave. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
A very dangerous illness arrived in Lagos on an aeroplane. A doctor called Dr Adadevoh worked out what it was, and she would not let the sick man leave the hospital, because if he had left, thousands of people could have got sick. Important people shouted at her on the phone. She still said no. She caught the illness herself and she died — but Nigeria was saved. She did her job properly when it was very frightening.
*Ask them:* **Has anyone ever tried to get you to do something you knew was wrong? What did you say?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Nigeria's Ebola containment is taught internationally as a model. One person's refusal, plus one prepared system.

- **CAUTION — Do not name the Liberian ambassador on air.** Sources differ on the identity and there is no reported Liberian rebuttal on record. Say "Liberia's ambassador to Nigeria," and attribute the account to the hospital's medical director — which is what it is: one side's testimony, never tested in court.

# DAY 14

## "The Man Who Prayed for a Rough Road"

**RUNTIME:** ~7 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 14 · Service & Compassion*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

Today I am going to tell you about a Nigerian who spent his life serving the poor, went to prison rather than flatter a dictator, and built a school so that children who could not afford one could learn.

And he did not believe in God. He said so, publicly, all his life.

I am telling you that up front, because I am not going to pretend otherwise — and because what Jesus did with exactly this kind of story is one of the most uncomfortable things in the Gospels.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Proverbs 19:17 (NIV):**
*"Whoever is kind to the poor lends to the Lord, and he will reward them for what they have done."*

**Psalm 119:68 (NIV):**
*"You are good, and what you do is good; teach me your decrees."*

### 3. THE STORY

**Tai Solarin** was born in Ikenne, Ogun State, in the early 1920s. He was principal of Molusi College and he resigned — because he refused to open the school day with hymns and prayers. He was an atheist and a secular humanist, arguably the most prominent one in modern African history.

On **27 January 1956**, he and his English-born wife **Sheila** founded **Mayflower School** in Ikenne — Nigeria's first deliberately non-denominational school. Its motto: *"Knowledge is Light."* He was its principal for twenty years.

He was detained again and again. Forty-eight hours in 1966, for criticising a state wedding during the civil war. Thirty-two days in 1974, for distributing anti-government leaflets. Around **eighteen months from March 1984**, under the Buhari–Idiagbon regime. **Amnesty International repeatedly named him a prisoner of conscience.**

And on **1 January 1964**, he published a New Year message that Nigerians still quote. Everyone else was wishing each other prosperity. He wrote **"May Your Road Be Rough."**

*"May you have a hard time this year, may there be plenty of troubles for you this year... our successes are conditioned by the amount of risk we are ready to take."*

He died on 27 July 1994. His epitaph reads: *"Here lies Tai Solarin, who lived and died for humanity."*

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: God is not embarrassed to shame His own people with an outsider.**

Jesus told a story about a man beaten and left by the road. A priest passed. A Levite passed. And the person who stopped was a **Samaritan** — to His audience, the wrong man, the heretic, the outsider. Jesus told it that way *deliberately*, to a religious crowd, and He ended it by saying **"Go and do likewise."**

He told another one about two sons. One said "yes, father" and did not go. The other said "no" and went. Then He asked: which one did the father's will?

So look at Tai Solarin honestly. A man who denied God built a school for the poor, went to prison rather than flatter power, and asked his countrymen to pray for a rough road instead of an easy one.

**Scripture says the tree is known by its fruit. If a man who denied God did this much — what does God expect of us who claim His name?**

That is the sermon. It is not a comfortable one, and it is not new. It is Luke 10.

**Truth Two: The most loving thing is sometimes the hardest thing.**

*"May your road be rough."* Every instinct in us says that is a curse. Solarin meant it as the kindest thing he could wish a young Nigerian.

And Scripture, awkwardly, agrees with him. *"Consider it pure joy... whenever you face trials of many kinds."* *"It was good for me to be afflicted."* We will meet that second verse again on Day 20.

Which means real service sometimes has to confront the thing causing the suffering, not only relieve the suffering. It is easier to give a man food than to ask why he has none. Both are needed. Only one of them gets you into trouble.

Psalm 119:68 says *"You are good, and what you do is good."* True service is not just social conscience — it is **imitation of God's actual character.** And His character includes both the mercy that feeds and the justice that confronts.

### 5. THE BUILD

Ask one hard question this week about a need you regularly meet.

If you give to a particular person or cause — good, keep giving. **And ask once: "why is this need here?"** You may not be able to fix it. Ask anyway. That question is the beginning of every reform in history.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, let my compassion be brave enough to confront what causes the suffering I am trying to relieve. And let no unbeliever ever out-love me. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
A man called Tai Solarin built a school so that children who had no money could learn. He was put in prison many times because he told the truth about bad leaders. Every New Year, everyone says "I wish you an easy year!" But he said something strange: "I hope your road is rough" — because he knew that doing hard things is how we grow strong.
*Ask them:* **What is something hard that you got better at, only because you kept doing it?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Every nation has a non-believer whose life shames the religious. Ask who yours is — and then ask what it should cost you.

**ACCURACY NOTE — TWO SERIOUS FIXES:**

- **DO NOT — "Man Die" IS NOT TAI SOLARIN.** The original draft attributed it to him. It is **Wole Soyinka's** 1972 prison memoir ***The Man Died: Prison Notes of Wole Soyinka***, written after his 22 months of detention. # PART FIVE: JUSTICE & RIGHTEOUSNESS

# DAY 16

## "Senior Advocate of the Masses"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 16 · Justice & Righteousness*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

There is a title in Nigerian law called Senior Advocate of Nigeria. It is the highest rank a lawyer can hold. One man was passed over for it for more than a decade — not because he was not good enough, but because he was too much trouble.

So Nigerian students gave him their own title instead. And he said he valued it more.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Isaiah 1:17 (NIV):**
*"Learn to do right; seek justice. Defend the oppressed. Take up the cause of the fatherless; plead the case of the widow."*

**Psalm 119:121 (NIV):**
*"I have done what is righteous and just; do not leave me to my oppressors."*

### 3. THE STORY

**Chief Gani Fawehinmi** was called to the Nigerian Bar in **January 1965** and opened his own chambers three months later.

Between 1965 and 2002, his chambers handled roughly **5,700 cases — and more than 1,500 of those briefs were taken free of charge.** He described his clients as "the poor, the cheated, the ignored, the oppressed and the persecuted."

Between **1969 and 1996 he was arrested and detained around forty times, across twelve different jails and detention centres.** His passport was seized about ten times. His home and chambers were searched over and over. He was detained under Gowon, under Buhari, under Babangida, under Abacha.

He kept a suitcase packed at all times. For detention.

He was first rejected for Senior Advocate of Nigeria in the 1980s, and passed over for well over a decade, on grounds everyone understood to be political rather than professional. So Nigerian students — Obafemi Awolowo University students are the group usually credited — started calling him **"Senior Advocate of the Masses."** It was not an official title. Nobody awarded it. He is widely reported to have valued it above the real one.

He was finally made SAN on 10 September 2001 — and marked it with a public rally at which he attacked the Nigerian state.

In 2008, he **rejected** a national honour, the Order of the Federal Republic, as a protest against Nigerian governance. He died of lung cancer on **5 September 2009**, aged 71.

He was a Muslim. Every day of his life.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Justice is a set of verbs.**

Look again at Isaiah 1:17: **learn. seek. defend. take up. plead.** Five verbs, no adjectives. God does not ask Israel to *feel strongly* about injustice. He gives them a work order.

We are extremely good at the feeling. Nigerians are among the most morally articulate people on earth about what is wrong with Nigeria. Isaiah is uninterested in that. *Take up the cause. Plead the case.*

And note what Gani Fawehinmi actually did: he built a large, commercially successful legal practice **in order to be able to give 1,500 briefs away.** Do not tell this as a story of a man who renounced money. Tell it as a man who **built capacity so he could donate it.** That is a more useful model, and it is more accurate.

**Truth Two: Doing right can be the very thing that puts you in danger.**

Psalm 119:121 is a strange, honest prayer: *"I have done what is righteous and just; do not leave me to my oppressors."*

He is not saying "I did right, therefore I am safe." He is saying "I did right, **and now I need protection**" — because doing right is precisely what attracted the opposition.

So do not teach young people that integrity guarantees safety. It does not. Gani Fawehinmi went to jail about forty times. What Scripture promises is not immunity. It is that **God is with the person who does right**, and that the account is settled eventually, sometimes not in this life.

He stood. He was vindicated. And Nigeria largely did not follow, in his lifetime. Hebrews 11 has a phrase for exactly that: *"these all... did not receive what was promised."*

### 5. THE BUILD

Find one injustice you already know about — in your workplace, your school, your street, your church.

You have probably discussed it. **This week, do one verb.** Write the letter. Make the report. Accompany the person to the office. Pay the fee. One verb.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, turn my sympathy for injustice into action, whatever the personal cost. And be near me when doing right makes me a target. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
There was a Nigerian lawyer called Gani who helped poor people go to court for free — over a thousand times. Powerful people did not like it, and they put him in prison again and again. He kept a bag packed by his door, ready, because he knew they would come back. He never stopped helping.
*Ask them:* **Have you ever seen someone being treated unfairly? What could you do next time?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Every legal system needs its Gani. Find out who does free legal work for the poor where you live — and support them.

- **CAUTION — Do NOT say he "never charged a fee."** He ran a large, successful practice and a profitable law-reporting business (he founded the *Nigerian Weekly Law Reports*). The 1,500+ free briefs were a deliberate tithe out of a real business. The accurate framing — a man who built capacity in order to give it away — is more useful anyway.
- **CAUTION — He was a Muslim.** If you praise him, do not imply otherwise. He is a good example of Nigeria's cross-religious civic tradition.
- **CAUTION — Say "about forty times."** "Around forty detentions across twelve jails, 1969–1996" is the best-documented formulation; higher figures circulate unsourced.

# DAY 17

## "Four Men and Nine Men"

**RUNTIME:** ~7 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 17 · Justice & Righteousness*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

This is the hardest day in this series, and I want to teach it in a way that will not take anybody's side unfairly — because in the place where this happened, there are two sets of grieving families, and only one of them is usually remembered.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Amos 5:24 (NIV):**
*"But let justice roll on like a river, righteousness like a never-failing stream!"*

**Psalm 119:137 (NIV):**
*"You are righteous, Lord, and your laws are right."*

### 3. THE STORY

**Ken Saro-Wiwa** was a writer and television producer. In **1990** he co-founded the **Movement for the Survival of the Ogoni People**, and Ogoni leaders adopted the **Ogoni Bill of Rights** — demanding a fair share of oil revenue, political autonomy, proper representation, and repair of the environmental devastation in Ogoniland.

On **4 January 1993**, MOSOP mobilised an estimated **300,000 Ogoni** in peaceful marches. By early February, Shell had withdrawn its staff from Ogoniland. It has not produced oil there since.

Now the hard part.

On **21 May 1994**, four prominent Ogoni chiefs — **Chief Edward Kobani, Chief Albert Badey, Chief Samuel Orage and Chief Theophilus Orage** — were beaten and killed by a mob at Giokoo. They were Ogoni. They belonged to the more conservative faction that Saro-Wiwa had denounced.

**Four men were really murdered. That crime has never been properly investigated. Their families are still alive, and they ask why nobody commemorates their fathers.** Say that on camera. It is true, and leaving it out is how a nation-building series turns into a partisan broadcast.

Saro-Wiwa was arrested and charged with **incitement to those four murders.**

He was tried before a special military tribunal, constituted under a 1987 decree, with a serving army officer sitting on the bench and no appeal to the ordinary courts. Defendants were held for months without charge and without lawyers. The independent trial observer, a British QC named **Michael Birnbaum**, concluded the proceedings breached both Nigeria's own constitution and international fair-trial standards. **At least two principal prosecution witnesses later swore affidavits that they had been bribed to give false evidence** — allegations that in some accounts involved Shell, which the company denies. Saro-Wiwa's own defence team withdrew in protest.

He and eight others were sentenced to death at the end of October 1995, and **hanged at Port Harcourt Prison on 10 November 1995.** His reported last words: *"Lord take my soul, but the struggle continues."*

**The day after**, on 11 November 1995, Nigeria was **suspended from the Commonwealth** — unprecedented.

And on 12 June 2025, President Bola Tinubu announced posthumous national honours for the Ogoni Nine and said he would grant them **a full pardon.**

Ogoni groups rejected it. Amnesty International said it fell "far short of the justice the Ogoni Nine need and deserve." Because a **pardon** presupposes guilt and forgives it. **Exoneration** declares innocence. As of today, no exoneration has been enacted.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Justice must run in every direction, or it is not justice.**

Amos does not ask for a puddle of justice. He asks for a **river** — *"a never-failing stream."* Something continuous, that flows everywhere, that does not choose its channel by whose side you are on.

Two wrongs happened in Ogoniland, and a truly just person names both. Four men were murdered and no one answered for it. Nine men were killed by a state that staged a trial instead of holding one. **If you can only see one of those, you do not want justice. You want your side to win.**

That is the hardest thing in this series and I will not soften it.

**Truth Two: Human justice is an imitation, not an invention.**

Psalm 119:137 says *"You are righteous, Lord, and your laws are right."* Our sense that the tribunal was a sham is not a modern opinion. It is an echo. We recognise a rigged trial as wrong because there is a standard outside us that we did not make.

And there is comfort in that for anyone waiting on justice that has not come. Thirty years on, no exoneration. No answer for the four chiefs. **But the account is not closed just because the court has adjourned.** Amos's river has not finished running.

### 5. THE BUILD

Today, deliberately learn one fact about a group you tend to hear only one side about.

Not to change your mind. To make sure your justice runs in every direction.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, do not let me calculate the cost of justice so carefully that I never pay it. And do not let me want justice only for my own side. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
*(Note: this day is heavy. For under-10s, consider using the Day 16 or Day 18 story instead, or this simplified version.)*
Some people in Nigeria said, "Our land and our rivers are being spoiled, and it is not fair." One of them was a writer called Ken Saro-Wiwa. He spoke up, and the government at that time did something very wrong to him. Being fair means listening to everybody's story — even the people we disagree with.
*Ask them:* **When two friends are fighting, why is it important to hear both of them?**

# DAY 18

## "The Question That Was Never Answered"

**RUNTIME:** ~7 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 18 · Justice & Righteousness*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

In October 2020, a generation of young Nigerians who had been told they were lazy, entitled and unserious organised themselves across this entire country in about ten days, fed each other, funded themselves transparently, and asked one question.

The question has still not been answered.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Micah 6:8 (NIV):**
*"He has shown you, O mortal, what is good. And what does the Lord require of you? To act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God."*

**Psalm 119:138 (NIV):**
*"The statutes you have laid down are righteous; they are fully trustworthy."*

### 3. THE STORY

**SARS** — the Special Anti-Robbery Squad — was created in late 1992 to fight armed robbery and kidnapping. It became notorious for the opposite. **Amnesty International's September 2016 report** documented torture, detention without trial, forced confessions, extrajudicial killings, extortion and rape. Young men were stopped for owning a laptop, driving a decent car, having dreadlocks or a tattoo.

In early October 2020 a video circulated, allegedly showing SARS officers killing a young man in Ughelli, Delta State. From around **8 October 2020**, protests spread nationwide and into the diaspora.

On **11 October**, the Inspector-General announced SARS was dissolved. Protesters had heard that before and kept going.

On **19 October**, Lagos State inaugurated a **Judicial Panel of Inquiry**, chaired by retired Justice Doris Okuwobi, with civil-society and youth members.

On **20 October**, a curfew was announced at about 1pm to take effect at 4pm — a few hours' notice. At around **6:45 in the evening**, at the **Lekki Toll Gate**, soldiers opened fire on protesters who were seated, waving the national flag, singing the national anthem.

What happened next is contested, and I am going to give you all of it.

**The Lagos State Judicial Panel** — a panel the Lagos government itself set up — reported on 15 November 2021. It found **nine protesters killed by gunshot at Lekki and four missing and presumed dead**, with 24 gunshot injuries. It found soldiers *"shot, injured and killed unarmed helpless and defenceless protesters, without provocation or justification."* It found ambulances refused access, CCTV footage tampered with, bloodstains cleaned, spent bullets picked up. It said the conduct *"could in context be described as a massacre."*

**The Lagos State Government's white paper**, on 30 November 2021, accepted 11 of 32 recommendations and **dismissed the finding that nine people were killed as "assumptions and speculations."** The federal Information Minister had already called it a "phantom massacre."

**Amnesty International** put the toll at at least **12 killed at Lekki and Alausa** that night, and at least 56 across Nigeria over the protests.

**Five years later, no soldier or police officer has been prosecuted.** In July 2024 the ECOWAS Court found the Nigerian government responsible for violations and ordered compensation. Lagos has paid at least ₦410 million to around 70 victims and families. Many bereaved families have received nothing.

And I must say this too: in the days after 20 October there was widespread arson, looting, prison breaks and attacks on police stations across Lagos and other states. **Police officers and civilians died in that violence as well.** Those are different events with different figures, and we mourn all of the dead.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Accountability is a biblical category, not a political one.**

Micah 6:8 puts **"act justly"** first. Before mercy. Before worship language. The demand that a nation answer for what it does to the powerless runs right through Scripture — Isaiah 10:1–2 against those who *"make unjust laws"*; Proverbs 31:8–9, *"speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves."* And Romans 13, read properly, is not only an instruction to citizens. It is a job description for the one who carries the sword.

So a generation demanding accountability was, whether it knew it or not, appealing to a standard older than any government. Psalm 119:138 calls those standards **"fully trustworthy."** Governments are not. God's standards are. That is why we can measure a government against them.

And notice this, because it matters in Nigeria: **mosques and churches both sheltered and fed protesters in October 2020.** On that street, that argument stopped.

**Truth Two: Do not skip the third clause.**

Micah says three things: act justly, love mercy, **and walk humbly with your God.**

We usually quote the first two and drop the third — and the third is the one that keeps the first two from turning into self-righteousness. A person can pursue justice with a proud, cruel, contemptuous heart. Micah forbids it. **Humility is not a soft addition to justice. It is what stops justice becoming vengeance.**

And I will be honest with you about how this story ends, because a series that told you #EndSARS was a victory would ring hollow to every young person watching. It did not produce prosecutions. It did not produce structural police reform. SARS was disbanded on paper and its officers were absorbed into other units.

**They asked the right question. The question has still not been answered.**

That is not a reason to stop asking. Amos's river, again.

### 5. THE BUILD

Learn one thing about how accountability actually works where you live: who your local councillor is, how to file a police complaint, what your state's Public Complaints Commission does.

Not to protest. To **know**. Most injustice survives because nobody knows the procedure.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, make me part of a generation that acts, not just a generation that feels. And keep me humble while I am demanding justice, so that I do not become what I am opposing. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
There was a group of police in Nigeria who were supposed to protect people, but instead they were hurting people — especially young people. So thousands of young Nigerians stood together and said, "This is not right, and it must stop." Standing up for people who are being treated badly is one of the things God cares about most.
*Ask them:* **If someone was being bullied and you were the only one who saw, what would you do?**

When the civil war ended in January 1970, people who had held Biafran currency and brought it in to be exchanged were given a **flat payment of twenty pounds**, regardless of the amount they tendered.

Twenty pounds. Chinua Achebe put it into a short story called *Civil Peace*, where the character calls it the "egg-rasher" — a corruption of *ex gratia*. It has never left the Nigerian memory.

And what happened next is one of the most remarkable stories of recovery anywhere in the twentieth century.

Now — one correction, because I want you to hear this properly. **Onitsha and Aba did not become trading cities after the war. They already were.** Aba was a British military post from 1901, on the railway to Port Harcourt from 1915, and an industrial town by the 1930s. Onitsha's market was famous enough in the 1950s to give its name to an entire literary genre — "Onitsha Market Literature." Onitsha Main Market was **destroyed in 1968.**

So the story is not *founding*. It is **re-building** — which is harder, because you know exactly what you have lost.

They rebuilt it bigger. Onitsha Main Market is widely described as the largest market in West Africa. **Ariaria International Market** in Aba, established in 1976–77 after a fire destroyed the old Ekeoha Market, now has **more than 37,000 stalls** across three local government areas, serving Cameroon, Gabon and Central Africa, and known for its shoemaking as "the China of Africa."

That took a generation. It also took the apprenticeship system we looked at on Day 4 — one man settling the next, over and over, for fifty years.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: The harvest is certain. The timing is not.**

Galatians 6:9 contains a phrase people rush past: **"at the proper time."** Paul does not promise a quick harvest. He promises a certain one — *if we do not give up.*

That conditional is the whole verse. The people who rebuilt Aba did not know it would work. They had no evidence it would work. They had a twenty-pound note and a burnt-out market.

Most of the people who give up are not defeated. They are **early**.

**Truth Two: Comfort comes from a promise, not from circumstances.**

Psalm 119:50 says *"My comfort in my suffering is this: Your promise preserves my life."*

Look where the comfort is located. Not in the suffering ending. Not in circumstances improving. In a **promise** — something said, that he decided to keep believing when nothing around him confirmed it.

That is a very specific spiritual skill, and it can be practised. It is the difference between "I feel better because things are better" and "I can keep going because God said something and God does not lie."

### 5. THE BUILD

Find one promise of God in Scripture that speaks to the exact thing you are currently enduring. Write it on paper. Put it somewhere you will see it every day for the rest of October.

Not a feeling. A promise. Psalm 119:50 in practice.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, when I am struck down, remind me I am not destroyed. Give me strength for the next step, not the whole road. Amen."*

After the war in Nigeria, families came home and found their houses and shops burnt down. Everyone was given the same small amount of money — the same, whether they had been rich or poor. And they started again. Today those same towns have some of the biggest markets in all of Africa. Starting again is not the same as failing.
*Ask them:* **What is something you had to start again after it went wrong?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Post-war reconstruction anywhere — Germany, Korea, Rwanda, Vietnam — is the same story: a generation that had no evidence it would work, and did it anyway.

# DAY 20

## "Shot on a Camcorder"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 20 · Perseverance & Resilience*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

In 1992, a Lagos trader had a warehouse full of blank VHS tapes he could not sell.

So he decided to put something on them.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — 2 Corinthians 4:8–9 (NIV):**
*"We are hard pressed on every side, but not crushed; perplexed, but not in despair; persecuted, but not abandoned; struck down, but not destroyed."*

**Psalm 119:71 (NIV):**
*"It was good for me to be afflicted so that I might learn your decrees."*

### 3. THE STORY

**Kenneth Nnebue** was a trader. Celluloid film — real cinema — was financially out of reach for a Nigerian in 1992. So *Living in Bondage* was shot on a **VHS camcorder**, in **Igbo with English subtitles**, and sold **direct to video.**

He hired a director named **Chris Obi Rapu** — who worked under the fake name **"Vic Mordi"**, his wife's maiden name, because he was Controller of Programmes at NTA at the time and was not allowed to freelance. Rapu has said he was paid **₦10,000** to direct it.

It reportedly sold **over half a million copies within weeks.**

Now, a correction, because Nigerians argue about this one publicly and you do not want to be on the wrong side of it. ***Living in Bondage* did not birth Nollywood.** Hubert Ogunde was making Nigerian films before it. Baba Sala. The Yoruba travelling theatre tradition. The Yoruba home-video pioneer Alade Aromire was working from around 1982. And Kenneth Nnebue himself had already produced a Yoruba-language home video before *Living in Bondage*.

Here is the accurate version, and it is still a great story: ***Living in Bondage* was the breakout hit that proved the direct-to-video model could make serious money — and that is what industrialised Nollywood.**

And the scale now: Nigeria's own **National Bureau of Statistics recorded 2,599 Nollywood films in 2020**, up from 700 in 2019. On 2023 figures the motion picture, sound recording and music sector was **₦1.62 trillion** of Nigerian GDP.

Twenty-seven years later, in November 2019, Ramsey Nouah directed a sequel — *Living in Bondage: Breaking Free* — which grossed **₦168.7 million** at Nigerian cinemas. Cinemas that did not exist in any meaningful way when the first one was made.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Build with what is in the warehouse.**

Nobody gave Kenneth Nnebue a film industry. He had blank tapes, a camcorder, actors nobody had heard of, and a country with no distribution system. **He did not wait for conditions to improve. He built with what was in the room.**

Nigerians are extraordinary at this. We call it "managing," usually with a shrug, as if it were a failure. It isn't. It is a national gift. The failure is when we use it as an excuse never to build the proper thing — Day 9's lesson. But as a starting move? Building with what is in the warehouse is exactly right.

2 Corinthians 4:8–9 is four pairs, and every single one has a *"but"* in the middle. Hard pressed **but** not crushed. Perplexed **but** not in despair. Struck down **but** not destroyed. Paul is not denying the first half of any pair. He is refusing to let it be the last word.

**Truth Two: Affliction is a teacher you would never have hired.**

Psalm 119:71 is an outrageous verse: *"It was good for me to be afflicted so that I might learn your decrees."*

He is not saying the affliction was good. He is saying **something was learned in it that would not have been learned anywhere else.**

Nollywood learned things in its poverty that a well-funded industry never learns: how to shoot fast, how to distribute without a distributor, how to make a story carry a film when you cannot afford spectacle. Those constraints became competences.

Look at your own constraint — the money you do not have, the platform you were not given, the door that stayed shut. Ask the Psalm 119:71 question: **what is this teaching me that comfort could not?** Not "why me." What is this teaching me?

### 5. THE BUILD

Name the resource you keep saying you need before you can start.

Now start a smaller version, today, without it. Not the whole plan. The version that fits in the warehouse.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, let me build in spite of my conditions, not wait for my conditions to improve. And teach me the things only hard seasons can teach. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
A Nigerian man had a big pile of blank video tapes he could not sell. So he thought, "What if I put a film on them?" He had no proper camera and no money — just a small home video camera. He made the film anyway, and it sold hundreds of thousands of copies, and it helped make Nigeria one of the biggest film-making countries in the whole world. You do not need everything to start. You need to start with what you have.
*Ask them:* **What could you make right now with things you already have at home?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Every creative industry that matters started with somebody using the wrong equipment brilliantly.

- **CAUTION — Do not say it "birthed Nollywood."** This is actively disputed **by Nigerians** — Punch columnist Tunji Ajibade has argued in print that the claim "is a fraud," on the grounds that it erases Hubert Ogunde, Baba Sala and especially the Yoruba home-video pioneer Alade Aromire (working from around 1982); actor Yemi Solade has made the same objection. And Nnebue himself had already produced a Yoruba-language home video (*Aje Ni Iya Mi*) before it. Say **"the breakout hit that industrialised Nollywood."**

It was never like-for-like. Say **"among the highest-volume film industries in the world"** and cite the NBS figure of **2,599 films in 2020**.

# DAY 21

## "Three Naira to Nine"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 21 · Perseverance & Resilience*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

At the Paris Olympics in 2024, Nigeria sent 88 athletes and won **zero medals.**

At the Paris Paralympics, weeks later, Nigeria won **seven** — and got a third of the money.

Nobody threw them a parade.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — James 1:2–3 (NIV):**
*"Consider it pure joy, my brothers and sisters, whenever you face trials of many kinds, because you know that the testing of your faith produces perseverance."*

**Psalm 119:143 (NIV):**
*"Trouble and distress have come upon me, but your commands give me delight."*

### 3. THE STORY

Here is the comparison, and you should sit with it.

| | Paralympics | Olympics |
|---|---|---|
| Total medals, all time | **87** (40 gold) | **27** (3 gold) |
| All-time rank | **37th** | 80th |
| Tokyo 2020 | **10 medals** | 2 |
| Paris 2024 | **7 medals** | **0** |

Nigeria first competed at the Paralympics at **Barcelona in 1992** — six male athletes, three golds — and has competed at every Games since. Fifty-eight of those 87 medals are in athletics; most of the rest are in powerlifting, and overwhelmingly by women.

Let me name one. **Lucy Ejike.** Powerlifter. Six Paralympic Games. Silver in Sydney 2000. Gold in Athens 2004, breaking the world record twice in one competition. Gold in Beijing 2008, world records on her first and second attempts. Silver in London 2012. **Gold in Rio 2016, where she set three world records in a single competition — 135kg, then 138kg, then 142kg** — and carried Nigeria's flag at the opening ceremony. Bronze in Tokyo 2020.

Twenty-one years. Six Games. Six medals.

And the money. For Paris 2024, the Federal Government allocated roughly **₦12 billion** across both teams: reportedly **₦9 billion for the Olympic contingent and ₦3 billion for the Paralympic team.** Three naira for every nine.

The team that got nine came home with nothing. The team that got three came home with seven medals.

And para-athletes who did not medal reportedly received **no compensation at all** from the Ministry of Sports — while team officials each received a thousand US dollars.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Trials produce something. They do not merely test something.**

James does not say trials *reveal* your faith. He says the testing of your faith **produces** perseverance. It manufactures something that was not there before.

And notice that he does not ask you to pretend it is pleasant. *"Consider it pure joy"* is a decision about how to regard the trial, not a claim about how it feels. Nobody enjoys the trial. James asks you to reckon it as gain because of **what it is making.**

**Truth Two: Two true things at once.**

Psalm 119:143 is one of the most emotionally accurate verses in Scripture: *"Trouble and distress have come upon me, **but** your commands give me delight."*

Both. At the same time. He does not say the trouble left. He does not say the delight replaced it. He holds both.

That is what perseverance actually feels like from the inside. Not the absence of pain — **joy that survives alongside it.** If you are waiting to feel fine before you decide you are persevering, you will wait forever. You are persevering right now, in the trouble, with the delight running underneath.

**One warning, though.** Do not let this become "they succeed because they are neglected." They succeed **despite** it, and the neglect has real costs — athletes going home with nothing. Admiring resilience must never become an excuse for the conditions that made resilience necessary. Celebrate the athletes. **Then fix the budget.**

### 5. THE BUILD

Find one person or group near you who is doing excellent work with almost no support — a school, a team, a small ministry, a carer.

Do one concrete thing for them this week. And tell somebody else about them. Half of neglect is simply invisibility.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, let my trials produce perseverance in me, not an excuse to quit. And open my eyes to the people around me doing great work that nobody is funding. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
Nigeria has some amazing athletes who compete in the Paralympics — sports for people with disabilities. They have won far more medals than Nigeria's other Olympic athletes, even though they get much less money and much less help. One woman, Lucy Ejike, won medals at **six** different Games — that is over twenty years of training! Sometimes the strongest people are the ones nobody is watching.
*Ask them:* **Who is someone at your school who works really hard but nobody notices?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Paralympic teams are under-funded almost everywhere relative to Olympic teams. Check your own country's numbers.

# DAY 22

## "The Market That Burned"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 22 · Perseverance & Resilience*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

On a Sunday in February 2023, the biggest market in Maiduguri burned down.

Maiduguri. A city that had already absorbed more than a decade of insurgency, and hundreds of thousands of displaced people. And then the market — the engine of the whole city's economy — went up in flames.

Eight months later it reopened.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Psalm 37:24 (NIV):**
*"though he may stumble, he will not fall, for the Lord upholds him with his hand."*

**Psalm 119:28 (NIV):**
*"My soul is weary with sorrow; strengthen me according to your word."*

### 3. THE STORY

**Maiduguri's Monday Market** burned on **Sunday 26 February 2023.** Thousands of traders lost everything in a night.

The rebuilding was not a spontaneous act of self-reliance, and I am not going to pretend it was. **Borno State released ₦2 billion** to a rebuilding committee, plus ₦1 billion in disaster relief. Governor Babagana Zulum reopened the market in **October 2023**. Over **eight thousand traders** were allocated shops with **two years' rent waived**, and ₦800 million was distributed to 2,825 traders who could not be accommodated.

Traders who had lost everything went back and started again — with a state that, in this instance, showed up and paid.

Now, honesty, because this region deserves it. The wider story of return in Nigeria's north-east is not a happy one. Camps in Maiduguri were closed and food assistance stopped; between May 2021 and August 2022, **Human Rights Watch documented over 140,000 people compelled to leave eight camps.** People were resettled into places without healthcare, schooling or sanitation. And the security picture has deteriorated badly — in September 2025, an attack on Darul Jamal in Bama local government killed at least sixty people, who HRW records had **returned from camps closed by the authorities a month earlier.**

So do not let anyone tell you the north-east is a finished story of triumph. It is an unfinished story of people getting up again in a place where getting up is still dangerous.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Stumbling is not falling.**

Psalm 37:24 does not promise the righteous will never stumble. Read it again: *"**though** he may stumble."* The stumble is assumed. What is promised is that it will not be the final word — *"for the Lord upholds him with his hand."*

There is a difference between a setback and an identity. The traders of Monday Market lost a market. They did not become people who used to trade.

Whatever went wrong in your life this year — the business, the exam, the marriage, the health — the question is not whether you stumbled. You did. The question is whose hand is under you.

**Truth Two: "Weary with sorrow" is not a lack of faith.**

Psalm 119:28 is the most emotionally honest verse in this entire series: *"My soul is weary with sorrow; strengthen me according to your word."*

He does not open with praise. He does not perform. He says: **I am exhausted and sad.** And *then* he asks for strength.

Nigerian Christian culture is sometimes hard on this. We are quick to tell a weary person to speak positively, to declare, to stop confessing negativity. Psalm 119:28 does none of that. The psalmist names the weariness out loud **to God**, and that naming is where the request for strength begins.

If you are tired today: you are not backslidden. You are at verse 28. **Ask for the strength from where you actually are.**

### 5. THE BUILD

Say the true thing to God today. Out loud, if you can.

Not the polished version. "I am weary with sorrow." Then ask, in the same breath, for strength according to His word. That is a complete prayer and it is in the Bible.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, uphold me when I stumble, so that stumbling is never mistaken for falling. My soul is weary — strengthen me according to Your word. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
In a city called Maiduguri, there was a huge market where everyone bought their food and clothes. One night it caught fire and burned down. All those traders lost everything. But they did not give up — everybody worked together, and eight months later the market opened again with over eight thousand traders back in their shops. Falling over is not the same as staying down.
*Ask them:* **When you fall over, who helps you up?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Market fires devastate informal economies across the developing world. What separates recovery from collapse is usually whether anyone shows up with capital.

# DAY 23

## "The Letter Nobody Acted On"

**RUNTIME:** ~7 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 23 · Humility & Learning*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

Twenty-one months before a building in Lagos collapsed and killed forty-four people, an engineering firm put its concerns in writing and walked away from the job.

The letter exists. It is dated. The work continued anyway.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Proverbs 16:18 (NIV):**
*"Pride goes before destruction, a haughty spirit before a fall."*

**Psalm 119:67 (NIV):**
*"Before I was afflicted I went astray, but now I obey your word."*

### 3. THE STORY

On **1 November 2021**, at about a quarter to three in the afternoon, a twenty-one storey building under construction on Gerrard Road, **Ikoyi, Lagos**, collapsed. **Forty-four people died** — the toll rose from 42 in the first days of the search to 44 by 8–9 November — including the developer himself, Femi Osibona of Fourscore Heights Limited. His body was recovered on 4 November.

Here is what came out afterwards.

In **February 2020 — twenty-one months before the collapse — Prowess Engineering Limited withdrew from the project.** Its Managing Director, Muritala Olawale, wrote to the owner limiting the firm's guarantee to the first two buildings and to work done up to the fourth floor of the third building, and citing the owner's failure to provide concrete cube tests at each stage.

Concrete cube tests. That is the routine procedure where you take a sample of the concrete you are pouring and crush it, to confirm it is as strong as the design assumes.

The firm withdrew. The work continued.

And the Lagos State Building Control Agency had approved **fifteen floors.** Twenty-one were built.

A Special Investigations Panel chaired by Toyin Ayinde reported on **5 January 2022.** Lagos State accepted 26 of its 28 recommendations, ordered the demolition of the remaining towers, directed the prosecution of Fourscore Heights and the forfeiture of the site, and directed the prosecution of **three town planners and civil servants** for lying under oath and producing fake approvals through a private consulting company.

Forty-four people. One letter. Twenty-one months.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Pride is not loudness. It is deafness.**

We picture the proud man boasting. Proverbs 16:18 describes something quieter and far more dangerous: *"Pride goes before destruction."* Before. It is a **predictor**.

Why? Because pride removes your ability to see the fall coming. It does not shout. **It just stops hearing.** It reclassifies the engineer's letter as pessimism. It reclassifies the person who disagrees as "not a team player." It reclassifies the regulator as an obstacle.

And by the time the correction arrives, it is no longer a letter. It is a building.

Ask yourself the only useful question here: **when was the last time somebody told me something I did not want to hear, and I actually changed what I was doing?** If you cannot remember, that is your answer.

**Truth Two: Sometimes humility only arrives through consequence — and that is a mercy, not a punishment.**

Psalm 119:67 is one of the most disarming admissions in the Psalms: *"Before I was afflicted I went astray, but now I obey your word."*

He is not proud of that. He is simply telling the truth: he did not learn it the easy way. The correction had to cost him something before he took it seriously.

Most of us are like that. Which means the small painful corrections in your life right now — the failure, the rebuke, the loss, the thing that humbled you in front of people — may be the cheapest lesson you will ever be offered.

**Pay attention to the cheap corrections, so that you never need the expensive ones.** Forty-four people paid the expensive one.

### 5. THE BUILD

Go to one person who has the standing to tell you the truth — a spouse, a colleague, a mentor, an older sibling — and ask them, directly:

**"What is the thing about my work, or my character, that you have been reluctant to tell me?"**

Then say nothing except "thank you." Do not defend. Do not explain. That is the whole exercise.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, keep me correctable. Let me never mistake my position for permission to stop listening. And let me learn from the small corrections so I never need the large ones. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
*(Note: mention the deaths gently or omit them for the youngest.)*
Some builders were building a very tall tower in Lagos. The engineers — the people whose job it is to check that buildings are safe — said, "This is not being done properly," and they left. But the builders carried on anyway. The building fell down. When someone who knows more than you says "stop and check," it is not because they don't like you. It is because they can see something you cannot.
*Ask them:* **When someone corrects you, what does your face do? What should it do?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Every major engineering disaster — Challenger, Grenfell, Ikoyi — has a memo. There is always a memo.

## "The Head of State Who Went Back to School"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 24 · Humility & Learning*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

There is a document in a British university archive that I think is one of the most extraordinary things any Nigerian has ever produced.

It is a PhD thesis. It was submitted by a man who, nine years earlier, had been the Head of State of Nigeria.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — James 4:6 (NIV):**
*"But he gives us more grace. That is why Scripture says: 'God opposes the proud but shows favor to the humble.'"*

### 3. THE STORY

We met **General Yakubu Gowon** on Day 11, at the end of the civil war, saying "no victor, no vanquished" — and we were honest about the fact that the follow-through is still argued about.

Today, the second half of his life.

On **29 July 1975**, while he was attending an OAU summit in Kampala, Uganda, Gowon was **overthrown** in a coup led by officers including Colonel Joe Garba. He was in another country when he stopped being Head of State.

Consider what a man in that position usually does. Plots a return. Sulks in exile. Writes bitter memoirs. Buys property.

**Gowon went to England and enrolled at the University of Warwick — as a student.**

First as an undergraduate in politics. Then as a doctoral student. And in **February 1984**, the University of Warwick awarded him a PhD for a thesis titled ***"The Economic Community of West African States: a study in political and economic integration."*** His supervisor was Ian Campbell.

That thesis is in the Warwick Research Archive Portal. **You can download it and read it.** A former Head of State of Nigeria, sitting in seminars, submitting chapters, being corrected by a supervisor, and being examined like any other candidate.

And in **1996**, during the Abacha years, he founded **Nigeria Prays** — a national prayer initiative he has continued to lead. He is alive today. He turned 91 in October 2025.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Humility is hardest in victory, and most revealing in loss.**

James 4:6 says *"God opposes the proud but shows favor to the humble."* Opposes is a strong word. It does not say God is disappointed by the proud. It says He sets Himself against them.

And notice the two moments in Gowon's life where humility was tested, because they are opposite tests.

**In 1970 he had won**, and he chose the language of reconciliation over triumph. **In 1975 he had lost everything**, and he chose to sit in a classroom rather than plot a return.

Most of us can manage one of those. Gracious in victory, bitter in defeat — or humble in defeat, insufferable in success. The person who does both has something real.

**Truth Two: You are never finished learning, and status is the enemy of that.**

Here is the sentence I want you to take away today: **the highest office he ever held did not persuade him he had nothing left to learn.**

Nigeria has a deep problem with this. We treat a title as a terminal qualification. Once you are "Chief," once you are "Doctor," once you are "Pastor," once you are "Oga" — the learning is supposed to be over, and being corrected becomes an insult.

Psalm 119:75 goes even further. *"I know, Lord, that your laws are righteous, and that in faithfulness you have afflicted me."* He receives the hard thing as an act of God's **faithfulness** — not as evidence of God's absence.

That is an extremely mature posture and it does not come cheaply. But it is available. And it is the difference between a setback that teaches you and a setback that only embitters you.

### 5. THE BUILD

Learn something this month that puts you back at the bottom of a ladder. Deliberately.

A language. An instrument. A skill your children already have. Something where you are visibly, obviously a beginner and somebody younger than you is better at it.

That experience is a spiritual discipline. Book it this week.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, teach me humility especially in the moments I have power, not only in the moments I lack it. And never let my title persuade me I have finished learning. Amen."*

# DAY 25

## "We Fixed the Tax. We Never Fixed the Dollar."

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 25 · Humility & Learning*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

In August 1983, the World Bank published a report on Nigeria that said, in effect: the oil money is not coming back to the level you are planning around, and you must change the structure of this economy now.

Four months later there was a coup. The report is still online.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Proverbs 1:5 (NIV):**
*"let the wise listen and add to their learning, and let the discerning get guidance."*

**Psalm 119:130 (NIV):**
*"The unfolding of your words gives light; it gives understanding to the simple."*

### 3. THE STORY

Oil prices softened from the second half of 1981. Nigeria's exports fell from **twenty-six billion dollars in 1980 to under eighteen billion in 1981.** Real GDP turned negative. The fiscal deficit blew out from 0.5% to 9.3% of GDP in a single year.

President Shehu Shagari's government responded — and it responded quickly. In **April 1982** it passed the **Economic Stabilisation Act**: import controls, exchange restrictions, higher tariffs, wage restraint.

Import volumes did fall. But GDP kept contracting — minus 0.6% in 1982, minus 4.9% in 1983. The deficit reached 10.8% of GDP. Inflation hit 39%. On **31 December 1983**, the government was overthrown.

Now here is the correction I have to make to how this story is usually told. **It is not true that they did nothing.** They acted within months. What failed was the **diagnosis**.

The World Bank report of **15 August 1983** urged something specific and unpopular: devalue the naira, and diversify. It warned that *"real oil earnings are unlikely to regain their 1980–81 levels."* Shagari's government chose administrative controls instead — import licences, exchange restrictions. It did not devalue. That came in 1986, under Babangida's Structural Adjustment Programme, and by then the adjustment was far more brutal than it needed to have been.

**They did something urgent, and they did the wrong thing, because they rejected the counsel that named the real problem.**

And has Nigeria learned? Here is the honest answer, in two halves.

**On government revenue — yes, substantially.** Oil fell from around 74% of federally collected revenue in 2010 to about 26% in 2024. The tax base was genuinely fixed.

**On foreign exchange — no.** In the fourth quarter of 2025, crude oil alone was **51% of Nigeria's exports**, and petroleum products together were around **83%.** Genuinely non-oil exports were under 17%.

**We fixed the tax base. We never fixed the dollar.**

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Even the wise must keep learning — and the danger is not ignorance, it is the wrong diagnosis confidently held.**

Proverbs 1:5 is remarkable because of who it is addressed to. *"Let **the wise** listen and add to their learning."* Not the fool. The wise. The people who already know things.

Because the most dangerous person in a crisis is not the one who knows nothing. It is the one who knows a lot, has a confident theory, and stops listening to the people telling him his theory is wrong.

That is a leadership warning, and it is a personal one. Where in your life do you have a confident diagnosis that you have never allowed anybody to challenge? About your marriage. About your finances. About your child. About why that relationship failed.

**Truth Two: Understanding is available to anyone willing to let the word unfold.**

Psalm 119:130 is one of the kindest verses in the Bible: *"The unfolding of your words gives light; it gives understanding to the simple."*

**To the simple.** Not to the expert. Not to the economist. God's word gives understanding to the person who has no credentials at all — provided they let it *unfold*, which is a slow word, a patient word.

Which means humility here is not "I know nothing." It is: **I will keep going back to the source, and I will let it correct me, however long that takes.**

### 5. THE BUILD

Write down one confident opinion you hold about something important — money, politics, a family situation, your own future.

Now go and find the best argument **against** it. Not a weak one. The strongest one you can find, made by someone who actually knows.

You do not have to change your mind. You do have to actually read it.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, give me ears that stay open to correction, even when I feel I have already arrived. Unfold Your word to me and give understanding, even to the simple. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
A long time ago, some very clever people warned Nigeria: "You are getting almost all your money from oil. If the oil price falls, you will be in big trouble — please start doing other things too." Nigeria's leaders were busy and they did something different instead. And then the oil price fell, and there was big trouble. Listening to a warning is not weakness. It is wisdom.
*Ask them:* **Has a grown-up ever warned you about something and you did it anyway? What happened?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Resource dependence has trapped countries on every continent. The warning is always available; the humility to act on it rarely is.

# DAY 27

## "I Will Not Accept an Inferior Position"

**RUNTIME:** ~6 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 27 · Excellence & Craftsmanship*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

In 1956, Queen Elizabeth the Second sat for a Nigerian sculptor.

He made her look African.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Colossians 3:23 (NIV):**
*"Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord, not for human masters."*

**Psalm 119:127 (NIV):**
*"Because I love your commands more than gold, more than pure gold."*

### 3. THE STORY

**Ben Enwonwu** — Odinigwe Benedict Chukwukadibia Enwonwu — was born in 1917 and is generally regarded as the father of Nigerian modernism. He trained at the **Slade School of Fine Art** in London, at the Ruskin School at Oxford, and studied anthropology at the University of London.

He was both a sculptor and a painter, and you need both halves to understand him.

Around her 1956 visit to Nigeria, he was commissioned to sculpt the Queen. **She sat for him in London**, and the bronze was unveiled at a Royal Society of British Artists exhibition in 1957.

And here is the thing. His biographer noted that the sculpture *"amalgamates the distinctive features of Queen Elizabeth with the serene expression of Enwonwu's Head of a Yoruba Girl."* His son put it plainly: *"the artist depicted the queen through his African eyes — the work had African features, which was characteristic of his style."*

Think about what that took. The most prestigious commission available to any artist in the British Empire — **and he did not flatten his own artistic language to please the sitter.**

He said it out loud, too. In a BBC interview in **1958**: ***"I will not accept an inferior position in the art world."***

His work **"Anyanwu"** — "Eye of the Sun" — stands in the United Nations Art Collection in New York. His **"Sango"**, made in 1964 for what became NEPA, is one of the most recognisable sculptures in Lagos.

And in 1973 he painted three portraits of an Ife princess, **Adetutu Ademiluyi** — the "**Tutu**" paintings. They vanished in 1975. One of them was **found in a London flat in 2017**, and sold at Bonhams in February 2018 for **£1,205,000.**

He died in 1994.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Your real audience is not the person paying you.**

Colossians 3:23 does two things in one sentence. First, *"whatever you do"* — it removes every excuse of low stakes. There is no task too small to be covered by this. Second, *"as working for the Lord, not for human masters"* — it **relocates your audience.**

That is enormously freeing and slightly terrifying. Freeing, because the client's opinion is no longer the final word on your work. Terrifying, because now there is no such thing as a job nobody will inspect.

Enwonwu had every commercial reason to give the most powerful woman in the world exactly what she and her court expected. He worked for a different audience.

**Truth Two: What you love more than gold determines what you will not do for gold.**

Psalm 119:127 — *"Because I love your commands more than gold, more than pure gold."*

Every craftsperson faces the same moment. The work is not quite right. Fixing it costs you time, and time is money. And there is a voice that says: nobody will notice.

What you do in that moment is decided in advance by what you love more. If you love the money more, you ship it. If you love the standard more, you fix it. **Excellence is not a decision you make at the deadline. It is a decision you already made about what you love.**

And note what Enwonwu refused: he refused to be **graded on a lower scale because he was African.** That is a nation-building act. Every Nigerian who does world-standard work, in Nigeria, without apology, moves the whole country's reputation half a millimetre.

### 5. THE BUILD

Find something you finished recently that you know is not quite right — and that nobody else has noticed.

Go back and fix it. That is the entire assignment, and it will cost you something, which is the point.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, let my work be good enough to sign my name to — because I am really signing Yours. And let me never accept an inferior standard because of where I am from. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
A Nigerian artist called Ben Enwonwu was asked to make a statue of the Queen of England — the most famous person in the world at that time. He could have made it look exactly like all the English statues. Instead he made it in his own African style, because he was proud of how Nigerians make art. One of his paintings was lost for over forty years, and when somebody found it in a flat in London, it sold for more than a million pounds.
*Ask them:* **What is something about being Nigerian that you are proud of?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Every artist from a country the art world once patronised has faced the same choice. Name one from yours.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Excellence is not only invention. It is also restoration.**

We tend to think excellence means making something new. Sometimes the harder and more valuable act is refusing to let something **already valuable** be dismissed as ordinary.

Adire did not need improving. It needed somebody to stand in front of it and say: this is not folk material, this is art, and I will not accept it being graded on a lower scale — the same thing Ben Enwonwu said yesterday, said with cloth instead of bronze.

Look around your own community for what is being dismissed as "merely local." A language. A food. A craft. A form of music. A way of building. **Somebody has to be the one who refuses the downgrade.**

**Truth Two: Mastery, rightly held, turns into praise.**

Psalm 119:171 — *"May my lips overflow with praise, **for** you teach me your decrees."*

The praise is *because of* the teaching. Learning something deeply and praising God are not two separate activities in this verse; one produces the other.

That is a real experience, and any craftsperson knows it. The moment you understand *why* the joint holds, why the dye takes, why the equation works — there is a response in you that is closer to worship than to pride. Psalm 147:11 says God **"delights"** in those who fear Him. Delight, on both sides.

And notice her method: **she gave it away.** Three thousand people, free. Mastery that ends with you is not mastery; it is a hobby with a reputation. **You have not truly mastered something until somebody else can do it because of you.**

### 5. THE BUILD

Teach one person one thing you are good at. This week. For free.

Not a course. Not a programme. One person, one skill, one sitting. That is how Nike Davies-Okundaye got to three thousand.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, give me eyes to see the value in what others have dismissed, and the excellence to restore it. And let me give away what I know. Amen."*

# DAY 29

## "What the World Now Concedes"

**RUNTIME:** ~7 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 29 · Excellence & Craftsmanship*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

I am going to say something today, and then I am going to say something else immediately afterwards, and I need you to hear both.

The first thing: the world's most exacting gatekeepers now grade Nigerian work at the very top of the global scale.

The second thing: I am not holding up the personal lives of the people who did it as an example to you. I will explain why I am saying it that way.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Proverbs 22:29 (NIV):**
*"Do you see someone skilled in their work? They will serve before kings; they will not serve before officials of low rank."*

**Psalm 119:172 (NIV):**
*"May my tongue sing of your word, for all your commands are righteous."*

### 3. THE STORY

Start with the hardest gatekeepers there are — the ones who do not care where you are from and cannot be persuaded by sentiment. Venues. Ticket sales. Streaming counts.

**In 2018, a Nigerian artist sold out The O2 Arena in London** — the first solo African artist to do it. In January 2019, another one did the same.

**On 3 June 2023, a Nigerian artist headlined London Stadium in front of more than sixty thousand people** — the first African artist ever to headline a UK stadium. Weeks earlier he had performed at the UEFA Champions League Final kick-off show in Istanbul, to a stadium of over seventy-one thousand. In July 2023 he sold out Citi Field in New York, around forty-one thousand people — the first African artist to sell out an American stadium. In April 2022 he had already headlined Madison Square Garden.

**On 11 September 2023, "Calm Down" by Rema became the first track led by an African artist to enter Spotify's Billions Club** — one billion streams. It passed two billion by July 2024 and went seven-times platinum in the United States.

And the Grammys. **Burna Boy won Best Global Music Album in 2021. Wizkid won Best Music Video** as a featured artist on Beyoncé's "Brown Skin Girl" the same night. **Tems won Best Melodic Rap Performance in 2023**, and won the new Best African Music Performance category in **2025**.

Now — the second thing I promised.

Some of the leading artists in this movement have public conduct I would not commend to you, and lyrical content I would not play in this room. That is a fact and I am not going to pretend otherwise, because half of you already know it and the other half can find out in ten seconds.

So hear exactly what I am claiming, and nothing more. **I am not telling you these are men to imitate. I am telling you what the world now concedes about Nigerian work.** Those are two entirely different statements.

And so that the point does not rest on music alone: **Wole Soyinka won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1986** — the first African laureate in Literature. **Ben Okri won the Booker Prize in 1991.** **Dr Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala** — Harvard, then a PhD from MIT — rose to Managing Director for Operations at the World Bank overseeing an $81 billion portfolio, and has been **Director-General of the World Trade Organization since March 2021**, the first woman and the first African ever to hold the post. And **Paystack**, founded in Lagos in 2015 by Shola Akinlade and Ezra Olubi, was acquired by Stripe in **October 2020 for over $200 million.**

That is the range. Literature. Trade. Technology. Music. **"Made in Nigeria" is now a claim about standard, not about origin.**

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: Skilled work does not stay hidden.**

Proverbs 22:29 is one of the few genuinely predictive proverbs. *"Do you see someone skilled in their work? They will serve before kings."*

It does not say connected. It does not say lucky. It says **skilled**. And it promises reach — that the work will end up in rooms its maker never imagined.

Nobody making a record in a Lagos bedroom in 2015 was planning on London Stadium. Nobody writing code in a Lagos flat in 2015 was planning on Stripe. **They were making the work good. The rooms came to find them.**

That does not mean skill is always rewarded. It means skill is the only part of this you control — so pour yourself into the part you control.

**Truth Two: The instrument is neutral. The direction is not.**

Psalm 119:172 — *"May my tongue sing of your word, for all your commands are righteous."*

The tongue is the most ordinary instrument any of us owns. It sings, it sells, it argues, it lies, it praises. The psalmist does not ask for a different instrument. **He asks for the direction of the one he already has.**

That is the whole tension of today. Nigeria has extraordinary creative power. Power is not the same thing as righteousness. A nation can be world-class and still be building nothing.

So the prayer for our creative generation is not "be less excellent." It is: **be excellent, and aim it somewhere worth aiming it.** Skill plus direction is nation-building. Skill without direction is just noise at high volume.

And here is the question I want to leave with anyone young watching this. You are almost certainly going to be more skilled than your parents' generation, with more reach than they ever had. **What will you point it at?**

### 5. THE BUILD

Take the thing you are best at.

Write down one sentence: **"I will point this at ______."** A person. A problem. A community. A wrong.

Keep the sentence. You will need it tomorrow.

### 6. THE PRAYER

*"Lord, let my pursuit of excellence not be for applause. Make me skilled — and then point me somewhere worth pointing. Amen."*

---

**FOR YOUNGER CHILDREN (6–10):**
Nigerian music is now played all over the whole world — in America, in England, everywhere. Nigerians have won the biggest prizes for writing books, for running world organisations, and for building computer companies. When something says "Made in Nigeria," people around the world now think, "That must be good." That happened because Nigerians did their work brilliantly.
*Ask them:* **What would you like the world to know Nigerians are good at?**

**BEYOND NIGERIA:** Every country has had a moment where the world stopped condescending to its work. Ask when yours was — or whether it is still coming.

# DAY 30 — CONCLUSION

## "The Nigeria God Intends"

**RUNTIME:** ~7 minutes
**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** *Day 30 · The Nigeria God Intends*

---

### 1. THE HOOK

Thirty days ago I told you that Nigeria's problem was never a shortage of resources.

I want to tell you now what I think it actually is, and then I want to ask you one question.

### 2. THE WORD

**Theme Scripture — Proverbs 14:34:**
*"Righteousness exalts a nation, but sin is a reproach to any people."* (KJV)
*"Righteousness exalts a nation, but sin condemns any people."* (NIV)

**Psalm 119:105 (NIV):**
*"Your word is a lamp for my feet, a light on my path."*

**A short note on this verse, because it will make you a better teacher of it.** The Hebrew word behind "reproach" is *chesed* — which almost everywhere else in Scripture means covenant love, steadfast kindness, mercy. Only here and in Leviticus 20:17 does it carry a dark edge. Some scholars say there is a second, look-alike Hebrew word meaning "shame." Others say Solomon is being deliberately ironic: **the "kindness" a nation boasts in, when it is not built on righteousness, is itself sin.**

Either way it lands in the same place. **A nation is not exalted by what it congratulates itself on. It is exalted by righteousness.**

### 3. THE STORY — ALL OF THEM

Let me put the whole month in one breath.

**Dora Akunyili** gave back twelve thousand pounds nobody knew about, and that is why she was in the room when Nigerians were dying of fake drugs.

**Six young corps members** ran to a police station in Giade and did not come out, because they had shown up to do an ordinary job.

**A young man in Nnewi** sold motorcycle parts carefully for years, and ended up building cars.

**Vaccinators walked to every village in Nigeria**, over and over, for nearly a quarter of a century, until wild polio was gone from this continent.

**Chief Awolowo** taxed cocoa farmers and put three hundred thousand extra children into classrooms in one year.

**Dr Adadevoh** picked up a phone, said no to an ambassador, and died of it — and twenty million Lagosians did not get Ebola.

**Tai Solarin**, who did not believe in God, built a school for the poor and went to prison rather than flatter a dictator.

**Zannah Mustapha** put the children of Boko Haram fighters in the same classroom as the children of their victims.

**Gani Fawehinmi** kept a suitcase packed by his door for forty detentions.

**Traders in Aba and Onitsha** were handed twenty pounds and rebuilt the largest markets in West Africa.

**Lucy Ejike** won medals at six Paralympic Games on a third of the budget.

**Yakubu Gowon** stopped being Head of State and went and sat in a classroom in England until they gave him a doctorate.

**Professor Francisca Okeke** became one of the best scientists in the world — at Nsukka.

**Ben Enwonwu** sculpted a queen and made her look African.

**Nike Davies-Okundaye** trained three thousand young Nigerians for free in a craft the world called "merely local."

Not one of those was a perfect person in a perfect country. **Every single one was an imperfect person choosing, in an unfair situation, to build rightly anyway.**

That is the whole curriculum. There is nothing else.

### 4. TWO TRUTHS

**Truth One: This was never theology. It was a job description.**

Integrity. Discipline. Wisdom. Service. Justice. Perseverance. Humility. Excellence.

Those eight words are not a poster. They are the actual mechanism by which a country either works or does not — and they are exercised in the smallest rooms. **A nation is the sum of how its people behave when nobody is checking.** That is why we started on Day 1 with the only piece of Nigeria you control.

**Truth Two: The lamp shows the next step. That is all it has ever shown.**

Psalm 119:105 has walked with us for thirty days, one verse at a time. And notice what it actually promises: *"a lamp for my feet, a light on my path."*

**Feet.** Not horizon. A lamp in the ancient world was a small clay oil lamp — it lit roughly the next step, and no further.

God has not promised to show you the whole of Nigeria's future. He has never promised that to anybody. He has promised **enough light for the next step**, and He has promised it every single day for the rest of your life.

Which means the question was never "how do we fix Nigeria." **The question is: what is the next step, and will you take it?**

### 5. THE BUILD — THE FINAL ONE

On Day 1, I asked you to write down one thing in your hand that Nigeria would be better off if you did with excellence.

Go and find it.

Now three questions, and I want honest answers, not impressive ones:

1. **Which of the eight values is your weakest?** Not your favourite. Your weakest. Name it out loud.
2. **What is one concrete thing you will do about it, starting tomorrow?** Not a resolution. A thing with a day and a time.
3. **Who will you tell, so that they can ask you?** Day 6 taught us — *taken, and confirmed.*

And if God asked you today, *"What did you build for My Nigeria this month?"* — what would your honest answer be?

### 6. THE CLOSING PRAYER

*"Lord, thirty days ago You reminded us that except You build the house, we labour in vain.*

*We have walked through Akunyili's integrity and Adadevoh's courage, Awolowo's foresight and Mustapha's mercy, Fawehinmi's stubbornness and Ejike's endurance, Gowon's humility and Okeke's excellence. In every one of them we saw not a perfect nation, but an imperfect people choosing, again and again, to build rightly in spite of everything working against them.*

*Make us that kind of people.*

*Not the Nigeria of our complaints — the Nigeria You intend. Righteous enough to be exalted, not condemned. And where we are scattered across other nations, let us build those nations rightly too.*

*Let this not end as thirty days of teaching. Let it begin as a lifetime of building.*

*In Jesus' name, Amen."*

None of them were perfect. All of them chose to do the right thing when it was difficult. God is not asking you to fix the whole country. He is asking you to take the next step, today.
*Ask them:* **What is ONE good thing you are going to keep doing, even after this month is over?**
